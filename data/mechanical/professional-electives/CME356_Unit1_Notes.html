1. Gas Turbine Applications and Operating Regimes

A gas turbine converts the chemical energy of fuel into useful mechanical power or, in some applications, thrust. The mechanical design of its rotating components depends strongly on how the machine is expected to operate.

Base-load operation

Base-load operation means the machine is intended to supply a relatively continuous demand for long periods. Design priorities include high reliability, durability, thermal stability, efficiency and long service life.

Peak-load operation

Peak-load operation is used when demand rises above the normal level. Frequent load changes and transient conditions make start-up/shutdown cycles, thermal stresses and response important.

Standby operation

A standby machine is normally available for operation when another source or machine is unavailable. Long idle periods may be followed by rapid starting, so availability and starting reliability are important.

Backup operation

Backup operation provides additional capacity during contingencies or failures. The design must consider readiness, safe start-up and the loads produced during the required operating period.

Base loadLong-duration reliability, efficiency, creep and fatigue life
Peak loadLoad variation, transients, thermal cycling and response

2. Why Rotating Machinery Experiences Damage

Gas-turbine components can experience high temperature, centrifugal force, pressure, vibration, thermal cycling and mechanical loading. Damage therefore depends on both the operating condition and the material/design response.

High temperature + sustained stress → Creep

Repeated/fluctuating stress → Fatigue

Combined thermal + mechanical loading → Complex damage interaction

3. Creep

Creep is the time-dependent permanent deformation of a material under sustained stress, particularly at elevated temperature.

Stages of creep

  1. Primary creep: creep rate decreases with time.
  2. Secondary creep: creep rate becomes approximately steady and is important for engineering life assessment.
  3. Tertiary creep: creep rate accelerates because of progressive damage and leads towards rupture.

Strain ↑

Primary → Secondary → Tertiary → Rupture

Time →

Factors affecting creep

  • Temperature.
  • Applied stress.
  • Exposure time.
  • Material composition and microstructure.
  • Grain structure and manufacturing condition.
  • Surface and environmental conditions.
Engineering idea: increasing temperature and/or sustained stress generally increases the creep rate and reduces useful creep life.

4. Fatigue

Fatigue is progressive damage caused by repeated or fluctuating loading. It can occur at stress levels below the static tensile strength of a material.

Typical fatigue process

1. Crack initiation — a local stress concentration, defect or surface condition provides a favourable site.
2. Crack propagation — repeated loading causes the crack to grow.
3. Final fracture — the remaining load-carrying section becomes insufficient.

Factors affecting fatigue

  • Stress amplitude.
  • Mean stress.
  • Number of cycles.
  • Stress concentration.
  • Surface finish.
  • Material microstructure.
  • Temperature and environment.
  • Residual stresses.
  • Component geometry and defects.

5. Creep vs Fatigue

FeatureCreepFatigue
Primary driverSustained stress, especially at high temperatureRepeated/fluctuating stress
Time/cyclesStrongly time-dependentStrongly cycle-dependent
Typical concernLong-duration deformation and ruptureCrack initiation and propagation
Important control factorsTemperature, stress, time, materialStress amplitude, mean stress, cycles, geometry, surface

In gas turbines, creep and fatigue may interact: high-temperature operation promotes creep while repeated start-stop and thermal cycles promote fatigue.

6. Material Parameters Affecting Creep and Fatigue

  • High-temperature strength: ability to carry load at service temperature.
  • Creep resistance: resistance to time-dependent deformation.
  • Fatigue resistance: ability to withstand repeated loading.
  • Fracture toughness: resistance to unstable crack growth.
  • Oxidation/corrosion resistance: ability to survive the operating environment.
  • Density: important because rotating components experience centrifugal loading.
  • Thermal properties: influence temperature gradients and thermal stresses.

7. Design Parameters Affecting Damage

  • Component geometry and thickness.
  • Stress concentration at holes, fillets, grooves and joints.
  • Surface finish.
  • Blade/disc dimensions and mass distribution.
  • Cooling arrangement.
  • Clearances and alignment.
  • Factor of safety and allowable stress.
  • Expected operating temperature and pressure.

8. Operational Parameters Affecting Damage

  • Rotational speed.
  • Load level and load changes.
  • Start-stop frequency.
  • Temperature and thermal cycles.
  • Overspeed events.
  • Vibration level.
  • Operating environment and contaminants.
  • Maintenance and inspection practices.

9. Experimental Characterisation of Creep

A creep test applies a controlled load or stress to a specimen at a controlled elevated temperature and measures deformation with time.

Basic procedure: prepare specimen → establish test temperature → apply specified stress → record strain versus time → identify creep stages → determine creep rate and life-related properties.

Important outputs can include creep strain, creep rate and rupture time under specified conditions.

10. Experimental Characterisation of Fatigue

A fatigue test subjects a specimen to repeated or cyclic loading and records the number of cycles required to reach a defined failure condition.

Basic procedure: prepare specimen → apply controlled cyclic stress → record cycles → identify crack/failure → repeat for different stress levels → construct fatigue data such as an S-N relationship.

11. Design Workflow for Rotating Components

1. Define operating regime and service life.
2. Determine temperature, pressure, speed and loads.
3. Select candidate material.
4. Estimate stresses and thermal effects.
5. Check static strength, creep and fatigue requirements.
6. Consider vibration, manufacturing and inspection.
7. Apply an appropriate safety margin and verify against applicable design criteria.

16. Quick Revision

Creep: time-dependent deformation under sustained stress, especially at elevated temperature.
Stages: Primary → Secondary → Tertiary → Rupture.
Fatigue: progressive damage under repeated/fluctuating loading.
Fatigue sequence: initiation → propagation → final fracture.
Design checks: strength + creep + fatigue + vibration + thermal effects + safety.

17. Exam Tips

  • Always distinguish time-dependent creep from cycle-dependent fatigue.
  • For creep, draw the three-stage strain-time curve in long answers.
  • For fatigue, explain initiation, propagation and final fracture.
  • For gas-turbine questions, connect operating regime to temperature, load, cycles and service life.
  • Use labelled engineering diagrams rather than long paragraphs wherever possible.

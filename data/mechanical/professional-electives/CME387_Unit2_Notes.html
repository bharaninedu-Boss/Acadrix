Unit II syllabus

The syllabus covers the principles, equipment, effect of process parameters, applications, advantages and limitations of Chemical Machining (CHM), Electro-Chemical Machining (ECM), Electro-Chemical Honing (ECH), Electro-Chemical Grinding (ECG) and Electro-Chemical Deburring (ECD). citeturn0search14

1. Chemical Machining (CHM)

Definition: Chemical machining is a material-removal process in which selected areas of a workpiece are exposed to a suitable chemical etchant while the areas that must remain protected are covered with a maskant.

Basic principle

The exposed material reacts chemically with the etchant and is dissolved. The maskant prevents the chemical attack on the protected regions.

Exam diagram: show maskant, exposed area and etchant clearly.

Typical process sequence

  1. Clean the workpiece.
  2. Apply the maskant.
  3. Cut/remove maskant from the areas to be machined.
  4. Expose the workpiece to the etchant.
  5. Control machining time and etchant condition.
  6. Wash, neutralize and remove the remaining maskant.
  7. Inspect the finished profile.

Important process parameters

  • Etchant type and concentration
  • Etchant temperature
  • Machining time
  • Agitation / flow condition
  • Workpiece material
  • Maskant quality and thickness
Etch factor = lateral etching / depth of etching

Exam note: Undercutting is caused by lateral attack below the maskant. Lower undercutting improves dimensional accuracy.

Advantages

  • No cutting force and therefore little mechanical distortion.
  • Complex and shallow shapes can be produced.
  • Useful for thin sections.
  • Multiple parts can be processed together.

Limitations

  • Material must be chemically compatible with the process.
  • Undercutting affects dimensional accuracy.
  • Handling of chemicals requires care.
  • Very deep cavities are generally unsuitable.

Applications

Used for thin sections, panels, small pockets, weight reduction features and selected aerospace/electronic components.

2. Electro-Chemical Machining (ECM)

Definition: ECM removes electrically conductive work material by anodic dissolution. The workpiece is connected as the anode (+) and the tool as the cathode (−).

Standard ECM answer diagram: tool = cathode, workpiece = anode, electrolyte flows through the gap.

Principle

ECM is based on electrolysis. When direct current passes through the electrolyte, metal is removed from the anode by electrochemical dissolution. The tool shape is reproduced approximately on the workpiece because material is removed without direct contact between tool and workpiece.

Equipment

  1. DC power supply
  2. Tool/cathode and workpiece/anode
  3. Electrolyte tank and pump
  4. Filtration and cooling system
  5. Tool-feed mechanism
  6. Control and circulation system

Working

  1. Place the conductive workpiece as the anode.
  2. Place the shaped tool as the cathode.
  3. Maintain a small controlled gap.
  4. Pump electrolyte through the gap.
  5. Apply DC voltage/current.
  6. Metal dissolves from the workpiece and reaction products are carried away by the electrolyte.
  7. Feed the tool to maintain the required gap and obtain the desired cavity/profile.
Faraday principle: material removal is proportional to the quantity of electricity passed through the electrolyte.

Factors affecting MRR

  • Current density
  • Electrolyte conductivity and concentration
  • Voltage
  • Inter-electrode gap
  • Electrolyte flow rate
  • Workpiece material and electrochemical properties
  • Temperature

Advantages

  • No mechanical cutting force.
  • No tool-work contact.
  • No tool wear in the conventional cutting sense.
  • Hard electrically conductive materials can be machined.
  • Complex cavities can be produced.
  • Good surface finish is possible.

Limitations

  • Only electrically conductive materials can be machined.
  • High initial equipment cost.
  • Electrolyte handling and disposal are important.
  • Dimensional accuracy is affected by the machining gap and electrochemical conditions.

Applications

Used for dies, cavities, turbine components, complex profiles, deburring and machining difficult-to-cut conductive materials.

3. Electro-Chemical Honing (ECH)

Electro-chemical honing combines electrochemical dissolution with a light mechanical honing action. The electrochemical action removes most of the material while the abrasive honing action helps remove the passive film and improves the surface.

Working idea

  1. Workpiece is made anodic and the tool/cathode is positioned with a small gap.
  2. Electrolyte flows through the working zone.
  3. Electrochemical dissolution removes material.
  4. Abrasive honing elements mechanically assist in removing the surface film and improve finish.

Advantages and applications

  • Good surface finish.
  • Useful for internal cylindrical surfaces.
  • Combines the benefits of electrochemical removal and honing.

4. Electro-Chemical Grinding (ECG)

ECG combines electrochemical material removal with the mechanical action of a rotating abrasive wheel. The workpiece acts as the anode; the conductive grinding wheel acts as the cathode and is electrically insulated except at the required working zone.

Process action

Most material removal is by anodic dissolution. The abrasive grains remove the surface film and provide limited mechanical cutting, so the process can machine hard conductive materials while reducing the mechanical load compared with conventional grinding.

Important parameters

  • Current density
  • Electrolyte type, concentration and flow
  • Wheel speed
  • Feed rate
  • Inter-electrode gap
  • Abrasive wheel characteristics

Applications

Useful for grinding hard conductive materials, tool sharpening and precision grinding operations where conventional grinding forces are undesirable.

5. Electro-Chemical Deburring (ECD)

Electrochemical deburring removes unwanted burrs from conductive workpieces by controlled anodic dissolution. The tool is shaped and positioned close to the burr so that the highest current density acts at the burr location.

Working

  1. Place the workpiece as the anode.
  2. Position the cathodic tool near the burr.
  3. Circulate electrolyte through the gap.
  4. Apply DC power.
  5. The burr dissolves preferentially because of the local current-density distribution.
  6. Flush away the products and inspect the edge.

Advantages

  • No mechanical deformation of delicate edges.
  • Several burrs can be removed in one operation when the tool is designed suitably.
  • Useful for inaccessible burr locations.

How to draw the diagrams in the exam

  • Draw only the important equipment blocks and the working zone.
  • Use straight lines and clear arrows for electrolyte flow.
  • Label anode (+), cathode (−), electrolyte and machining gap in ECM-based diagrams.
  • For CHM, clearly show maskant, exposed area and etchant.
  • Do not crowd the diagram with unnecessary decorative details.

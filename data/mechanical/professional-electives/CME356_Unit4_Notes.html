CME356 — Unit IV: Blade Design

R-2021 • Rotating Machinery Design • Exam-oriented notes

Official syllabus scope: discs and blades; stresses in conventional axial-flow blades; simple desktop method; leaning effects; flanges and bolted structures; leakage through flanged joints; fatigue failure.

1. Role of Discs and Blades

In an axial-flow gas turbine, blades extract energy from the flowing gas and transfer it to the rotor. The disc carries the blades and transmits torque to the shaft. Both components experience high rotational speed, temperature and mechanical loading.

Gas flow → Blade → Blade attachment → Disc → Shaft → Useful power

2. Main Blade Loads

  • Centrifugal force: caused by blade mass rotating at high speed.
  • Aerodynamic force: caused by gas pressure and velocity changes.
  • Thermal loading: caused by high temperature and temperature gradients.
  • Vibratory loading: caused by periodic excitation and flow disturbances.
  • Contact/attachment loads: transferred through the blade root.

3. Centrifugal Stress in a Blade

A blade rotates about the engine axis, so each blade section must provide the centripetal force needed to keep the outboard mass in circular motion. The corresponding internal tensile loading is a major design consideration.

Fc = mω²r

For a real blade with distributed mass, the force is evaluated by integrating the mass distribution. The designer must consider the variation of area, density and radius along the blade.

4. Aerodynamic Bending

Gas forces act on the blade airfoil and produce bending moments. The bending stress depends on the force distribution, distance from the neutral axis and blade section geometry.

σb = My/I

Here M is bending moment, y is distance from the neutral axis and I is the second moment of area.

5. Torsion of Blades

The aerodynamic force does not necessarily pass through the blade's shear centre or torsional axis. This can create twisting. Blade shape, twist distribution and attachment geometry therefore affect torsional behaviour.

6. Thermal Stress

When a blade experiences a temperature gradient, different regions tend to expand by different amounts. If expansion is restrained, thermal stress develops.

For simple restrained elastic expansion: σth ≈ EαΔT

This expression is an idealised relation. Real blade temperature fields and restraints require detailed thermo-mechanical analysis.

7. Simple Desktop Method

The simple desktop approach provides a practical preliminary design procedure using simplified geometry, loading and stress calculations before detailed numerical analysis.

Step 1: Define blade speed, temperature, material and geometry.
Step 2: Estimate blade mass and centrifugal load.
Step 3: Determine aerodynamic loading and bending moment.
Step 4: Calculate critical-section stresses.
Step 5: Combine relevant stresses and apply design criteria.
Step 6: Check vibration, fatigue, creep and attachment requirements.

8. Blade Leaning Effects

Blade leaning means the blade stack is displaced from a simple radial arrangement along the span. Lean can influence aerodynamic loading, load distribution, centrifugal forces and local stresses.

  • May alter gas-flow characteristics.
  • Changes the geometric load path.
  • Can influence stress distribution and vibration behaviour.
  • Must be evaluated together with manufacturability and clearances.

9. Flanges and Bolted Structures

Flanges provide detachable joints between casing or structural components. Bolted joints must safely transmit separating forces and maintain adequate clamping under thermal and mechanical loading.

  • Bolt preload creates clamping force.
  • External loads redistribute load between bolt and joint members.
  • Temperature differences can change preload.
  • Joint stiffness and contact surfaces influence load sharing.
  • Thread and local stress concentrations must be considered.

10. Leakage Through Flanged Joints

A flanged joint must maintain sufficient contact pressure to prevent unacceptable leakage of working fluid. Leakage can result from inadequate preload, distortion, surface imperfections, thermal deformation or gasket/seal problems.

Good joint design: sufficient preload + controlled deformation + suitable sealing surface + thermal compatibility + correct tightening procedure.

11. Fatigue Failure of Blades

Blades are exposed to repeated aerodynamic excitation, start-stop cycles, thermal cycling and vibratory stress. Fatigue cracks can initiate at stress concentrations, surface defects or attachment regions.

Repeated excitation → local cyclic stress → crack initiation → crack growth → possible fracture

Fatigue resistance is improved by good surface finish, suitable material, smooth transitions, control of vibration, appropriate stress levels and effective inspection.

12. Blade Root and Attachment

The blade root transfers centrifugal and aerodynamic loads to the disc. The attachment must prevent excessive movement while allowing the intended assembly and thermal behaviour.

  • High local stresses can occur at root features.
  • Contact pressure must be controlled.
  • Fretting can contribute to damage in contacting regions.
  • Manufacturing tolerances influence load distribution.

13. Combined Stress Check

A blade may experience centrifugal tension, bending stress, thermal stress and vibratory stress at the same time. The critical location and load combination should be identified rather than checking each load independently only.

σcombined = σcentrifugal ± σbending ± σthermal ± σvibratory

The signs and combination depend on the actual stress state and location.

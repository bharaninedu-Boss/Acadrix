CME356 — Unit III: Failure Criteria

1. Proof Strength and Ultimate Strength

Proof strength is a specified stress used to indicate the onset of a defined permanent strain when a distinct yield point is not available. Ultimate tensile strength is the maximum engineering tensile stress reached during a tensile test.

2. Failure Under Biaxial Stress

Rotating machinery may experience two principal stresses simultaneously. Failure theories provide criteria for deciding when the stress state becomes unacceptable.

Maximum principal stress theory

Failure is predicted when the largest principal stress reaches the relevant uniaxial limiting strength.

Maximum shear stress theory

Failure is associated with the maximum shear stress reaching the shear-yield limit.

τmax = (σmax − σmin)/2

Distortion-energy theory

For ductile materials, yielding is associated with distortion energy. Under plane stress:

σe = √(σ1² − σ1σ2 + σ2²)

3. Creep as a Failure Mechanism

Creep is time-dependent deformation under sustained stress, especially at elevated temperature. In rotating machinery it can change clearances, distort components and lead toward rupture.

  • Temperature strongly influences creep rate.
  • Stress and exposure time influence accumulated strain.
  • Material microstructure and heat treatment affect resistance.
  • Creep can interact with fatigue during thermal cycling.

4. Fatigue Properties

Important fatigue quantities include stress amplitude, mean stress, stress ratio and number of cycles to failure.

σa = (σmax − σmin)/2
σm = (σmax + σmin)/2
R = σmin/σmax

5. S-N Diagram

An S-N diagram relates cyclic stress level to number of cycles to failure and is widely used for high-cycle fatigue assessment.

Stress amplitude ↑
│\
│ \
│ \
│ \________
└──────────────→ Number of cycles, N

6. R-M Diagram

The R-M diagram represents fatigue strength in relation to stress-ratio/mean-stress conditions. In an examination, state the axes and definitions used by the prescribed course/reference.

7. Stress Concentration

Stress concentration occurs when geometry causes local stress to exceed nominal stress. Typical locations include holes, keyways, grooves, shoulders, threads and sharp corners.

Kt = σmax/σnom

Smooth fillets, suitable radii and careful geometry can reduce concentration.

8. Mean Stress Effect

Fatigue strength depends on alternating stress and mean stress. A tensile mean stress is generally more damaging than a compressive mean stress for many metallic components.

9. Goodman Diagram

The Goodman relation is a practical mean-stress correction used in fatigue design.

σa/Se + σm/Sut ≤ 1/n

10. Cumulative Fatigue Damage

A common linear cumulative-damage model is:

D = Σ (ni/Ni)

Here ni is the number of cycles actually experienced at stress level i and Ni is the cycles to failure at that level.

11. Safety Factor

The safety factor provides a margin between design demand and limiting capacity. Its definition depends on the selected design criterion.

12. Larson-Miller Time-Temperature Parameter

PLM = T(C + log10 tr)

T is absolute temperature, tr is rupture time and C is an empirical material constant.

13. Worked Mini-Example — Goodman Check

Given: σa = 80 MPa, σm = 40 MPa, Se = 240 MPa, Sut = 600 MPa.
Step 1: 80/240 + 40/600 = 0.333 + 0.067 = 0.400.
Step 2: 0.400 = 1/n, therefore n = 1/0.400 = 2.5.
Conclusion: Under the stated simplified Goodman model, the factor of safety is 2.5.

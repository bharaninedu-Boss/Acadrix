1. Introduction to Additive Manufacturing

Additive manufacturing (AM) builds a component by adding material according to digital geometry, generally layer by layer. Unlike conventional subtractive manufacturing, the design is not limited in the same way by cutting-tool access and material-removal requirements.

Exam definition: Additive manufacturing is a manufacturing approach in which a physical part is produced from digital model data by progressively adding material to form the required geometry.

2. DFMA Concepts and Objectives

Design for Manufacturing and Assembly (DFMA) combines manufacturing and assembly considerations during product design. In AM, the designer should exploit AM capabilities while considering process limits, build orientation, support strategy, post-processing and assembly.

Manufacturing objective

  • Make the geometry printable and manufacturable.
  • Reduce unnecessary post-processing.
  • Use material efficiently.
  • Control build time and quality.

Assembly objective

  • Reduce unnecessary parts.
  • Make interfaces simple.
  • Use AM for part consolidation where appropriate.
  • Prevent incorrect assembly.

3. Unique Capabilities of AM

  • Production of complex geometries that may be difficult to make conventionally.
  • Internal channels and cavities can be incorporated where the selected process permits.
  • Part consolidation can reduce the number of separate components.
  • Lightweight structures and material-efficient forms can be explored.
  • Customization and low-volume production can be practical.
  • Complex external features can often be produced without dedicated cutting-tool paths.

4. Exploring Design Freedoms

Design freedom should be used for a functional reason, not simply to make geometry complicated. The designer can explore lightweight structures, integrated features, curved passages, customized interfaces and consolidated assemblies when the AM process can reliably produce them.

Functional freedom

Integrate channels, guides, mounts or other features into the component where useful.

Geometric freedom

Use complex curves, internal features and non-traditional forms when they improve function.

Assembly freedom

Combine several components into fewer printable parts when this reduces assembly effort.

5. Design Tools for AM

AM design work commonly starts from a 3D CAD model and then considers the selected AM process, orientation, supports and manufacturability. The exact software is process-dependent.

  1. Create or modify the 3D model.
  2. Check geometry and required features.
  3. Select a suitable build orientation.
  4. Identify support requirements and difficult regions.
  5. Prepare the model for the chosen AM process.
  6. Inspect the final design and revise where necessary.

6. Part Orientation

Part orientation is the position of the component relative to the build direction. It influences support requirement, surface quality, build time, feature direction and post-processing.

Orientation considerationDesign effect
Support volumeMore support can increase material use and removal work.
Critical surfacesOrientation can influence their achievable quality.
Build heightGreater build height can increase build time.
Functional directionFeature and load orientation should be considered with the process.

7. Removal of Supports

Supports may be required for overhangs or other geometry that cannot be reliably built without temporary material. A good DfAM design tries to reduce support dependence when the selected process permits.

  • Orient critical features to reduce difficult overhangs.
  • Place supports where removal is accessible.
  • Avoid trapping support material inside inaccessible cavities.
  • Consider the surface finish after support removal.
  • Balance support reduction against build quality and functional requirements.

8. Hollowing Out Parts

Hollowing can reduce material use and mass when the component's function permits it. The designer must consider structural integrity, manufacturing constraints and access for any required post-processing or removal of unused material.

Do not hollow blindly: a lighter part is not automatically a better part. Check stiffness, strength, minimum wall requirements, manufacturability and cleaning/access needs.

9. Undercuts and Other Manufacturing Constraints

AM permits many geometries that are difficult for conventional machining, but the chosen AM process still has constraints. Layer formation, overhang limits, minimum feature size, thermal behaviour, powder or material removal and support access can restrict the design.

  • Identify process-specific minimum feature sizes.
  • Check overhang and support requirements.
  • Ensure enclosed regions can be processed and cleaned as required.
  • Avoid inaccessible post-processing features.
  • Consider dimensional accuracy and surface requirements.

10. Interlocking Features

Interlocking features can allow multiple elements to be produced as an assembly or constrained mechanism. Clearance and build strategy must be considered so the intended movement or separation is possible.

11. Reduction of Part Count in an Assembly

One major DfAM opportunity is part consolidation: several conventionally separate components may be redesigned as one AM component when the resulting geometry is manufacturable and functionally sound.

Before consolidationAfter consolidation
Many individual partsFewer integrated parts
More fasteners and interfacesFewer assembly interfaces
Separate manufacturing operationsPotentially one AM build plus post-processing
More assembly stepsSimplified assembly
Exam point: Part-count reduction is valuable only when the consolidated part remains manufacturable, inspectable, serviceable and economically justified.

12. Identification of Markings / Numbers

Identification features such as part numbers, serial information or orientation marks can be incorporated into the design when the selected AM process can reproduce them with the required readability and durability.

  • Place markings where they remain visible after assembly.
  • Provide sufficient feature size for reliable readability.
  • Consider whether the marking should be raised or recessed.
  • Avoid placing identification where post-processing will remove it.

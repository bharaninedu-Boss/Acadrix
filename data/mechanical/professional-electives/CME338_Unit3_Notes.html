Value Engineering Techniques

Exam-friendly notes on the creativity and decision-making techniques prescribed for Unit III.

1. Creativity in Value Engineering

Creativity is the ability to generate useful alternative ideas for performing the required function. In VE, the aim is not simply to make a product cheaper; it is to find better ways of achieving required functions while maintaining required performance, quality and reliability.

Basic rules

  • Separate idea generation from idea evaluation.
  • Encourage many alternatives before selecting one.
  • Question existing methods and assumptions.
  • Record even unusual ideas for later evaluation.

VE sequence: identify the function → generate alternatives → evaluate alternatives → develop the best alternative.

2. Brainstorming

Brainstorming is a group creativity technique used to generate a large number of ideas in a short time. During the idea-generation stage, criticism is deliberately postponed.

Rules

  1. Do not criticize or evaluate ideas while generating them.
  2. Encourage free and unconventional ideas.
  3. Aim for quantity first.
  4. Combine and improve ideas suggested by others.
  5. Record all relevant ideas for later evaluation.

VE use: find alternative materials, processes, layouts, manufacturing methods or ways of achieving the same function.

3. Gordon Technique

The Gordon technique is a creativity method in which the problem is deliberately stated in a more general or abstract form. The team develops solutions to that general problem first; the specific application is revealed or refined later.

Why it helps

  • Reduces fixation on the existing design.
  • Encourages fundamentally different solutions.
  • Helps the team think in terms of functions rather than existing components.

Example: Instead of asking “How can this particular clamp be redesigned?”, a team may ask “How can two parts be held together?” and later adapt useful ideas to the actual product.

4. Morphological Analysis

Morphological analysis systematically breaks a problem into important functions or parameters and lists alternative ways of satisfying each one. Alternatives are then combined to create possible concepts.

Different combinations can be screened against performance, cost, reliability, safety and other requirements.

5. ABC Analysis

ABC analysis classifies items according to their relative contribution to a selected measure, commonly cost or annual usage value. In VE it can help the team focus attention on high-impact items rather than treating every item equally.

  • A: relatively few items with high cumulative impact.
  • B: intermediate group.
  • C: many items with comparatively low cumulative impact.

VE purpose: prioritize investigation where meaningful cost or value improvement may be possible.

Exam point: ABC classification is a prioritization tool. The exact percentage boundaries are not universal; they depend on the application.

6. Probabilistic Approach

When the outcome of an alternative is uncertain, VE decisions can consider possible outcomes together with their probabilities. This prevents a decision from being based only on one assumed result.

For discrete outcomes, the expected value can be represented as:

Expected value = Σ (Probability of outcome × Value of outcome)

The alternative with the highest expected value is not automatically the final choice; risk, reliability, safety, quality, strategic factors and other constraints must also be considered.

7. Make-or-Buy Decisions

A make-or-buy decision determines whether a component or service should be produced internally or purchased from an outside supplier.

Factors to compare

  • Relevant internal production cost
  • Supplier purchase price
  • Capacity and opportunity cost
  • Quality and reliability
  • Delivery and supply risk
  • Special tooling or investment
  • Confidentiality and strategic importance
Important: Do not compare only the supplier price with total accounting cost. The decision should use relevant costs and practical constraints.

8. Function Cost Worth Analysis (FCWA)

FCWA compares the cost currently associated with a function with the worth of performing that function. It helps identify functions where a large gap may indicate an opportunity for value improvement.

Value improvement opportunity → Cost of function > Worth of function

Typical steps:

  1. Identify and define functions.
  2. Determine the cost allocated to each function.
  3. Estimate the worth required to reliably perform each function.
  4. Compare cost and worth.
  5. Investigate high-cost or poor-value functions.
  6. Generate and evaluate alternatives.

9. FAST — Function Analysis System Technique

FAST is a function-analysis technique that arranges functions in a logical relationship so the team can understand what the system must accomplish and explore alternatives.

  • Functions are normally expressed using an active verb + measurable noun.
  • The diagram helps expose relationships between functions.
  • It supports creative thinking by shifting attention from existing hardware to required functions.

Simple thinking pattern: “Why?” moves toward the higher-level purpose; “How?” moves toward the means of accomplishing a function.

FAST is a function-analysis tool, not merely a flowchart of physical components.

10. Break-Even Analysis

Break-even analysis determines the activity level at which total revenue equals total cost. It is useful when comparing alternatives with different fixed and variable costs.

Break-even quantity, Q = Fixed Cost / (Selling Price per unit − Variable Cost per unit)

At break-even: Total Revenue = Total Cost. Above the break-even quantity, contribution begins to cover profit; below it, the operation has a loss under the stated assumptions.

VE use: compare manufacturing alternatives, equipment choices or make-or-buy options when their cost structures differ.

11. Life-Cycle Cost (LCC)

Life-cycle cost considers the costs associated with an item over its useful life rather than looking only at its initial purchase or manufacturing cost.

Typical cost elements

  • Initial acquisition/design cost
  • Manufacturing or installation cost
  • Operation cost
  • Energy or consumable cost
  • Maintenance and repair cost
  • Downtime-related cost where relevant
  • Disposal, replacement or end-of-life cost
LCC ≈ Initial cost + Operating cost + Maintenance cost + Other relevant life-cycle costs − applicable residual value

Key idea: a higher initial cost can be justified when it produces sufficiently lower operating and maintenance costs over the life of the product.

2-Mark Quick Revision

  • Brainstorming: group technique for generating many ideas while postponing criticism.
  • Gordon technique: generalizes the problem to encourage less-fixed solutions.
  • Morphological analysis: combines alternative solutions across selected parameters.
  • ABC analysis: classifies items by relative importance/impact for prioritization.
  • FCWA: compares function cost with function worth.
  • FAST: Function Analysis System Technique.
  • Break-even point: point where total revenue equals total cost.
  • LCC: cost assessment over the useful life of an item.

CME356 — Unit II: Designing Forces

R-2021 • Rotating Machinery Design • Exam-oriented notes

2 Marks12 Marks

Official syllabus scope: rotational inertia, flight, precession of shafts, pressure gradient, torsion, seizure, blade release, engine mountings and bearings, and major loadings of rotating and pressure-casing components.

1. Loads in Rotating Machinery

Rotating components are designed for simultaneous mechanical, thermal and aerodynamic loads. The designer must identify each load, determine its direction and magnitude, combine critical load cases and check the resulting stresses and deformation.

Speed + mass → centrifugal/inertial load
Torque → torsional load
Pressure difference → pressure load
Unbalance → bearing and shaft loads

2. Rotational Inertia

Rotational inertia is the resistance of a rotating body to a change in angular velocity. It is represented by the mass moment of inertia.

I = ∫ r² dm    and    T = Iα

Here I is mass moment of inertia, r is distance from the rotation axis, T is accelerating torque and α is angular acceleration.

During acceleration and deceleration, the required torque produces torsional loading in shafts and torque reactions in connected components.

3. Centrifugal and Flight Loads

A rotating mass experiences an outward centrifugal force. For an element of mass rotating at angular velocity ω at radius r:

F = mω²r

For a distributed component, the total force is obtained by integration. This force is particularly important in turbine blades, discs, rotors and other high-speed parts.

Flight loading refers to loads associated with the operating environment of an engine when used in flight. In an aircraft application, manoeuvre, acceleration, vibration and installation conditions can add external loads to the rotating machinery.

4. Precession of Shafts

A rotating shaft whose spin axis changes direction experiences gyroscopic effects. This motion is called precession.

Centrifugal/gyroscopic effects increase with rotational speed and angular rate of precession.

The resulting gyroscopic moment acts on bearings, supports and the shaft system. It must be considered where the rotating assembly can change orientation or where aircraft manoeuvres impose angular motion.

5. Pressure Gradient Loading

Gas-turbine components are exposed to pressure differences across blades, discs, seals and casings. A pressure difference acting over an area creates a force.

F = Δp A

where Δp is pressure difference and A is effective area. The actual loading can be distributed and may produce bending as well as direct force.

6. Torsional Loading

Torsion occurs when torque is transmitted through a shaft. For a circular shaft in elastic torsion:

τ = Tr / J
θ = TL / GJ

Here τ is shear stress, T is torque, r is radial position, J is polar second moment of area, θ is angle of twist, L is shaft length and G is shear modulus.

High torque, small diameter, stress concentrations and sudden torque changes can increase torsional risk.

7. Seizure

Seizure is a severe condition in which relative motion between contacting components is lost because of excessive friction, overheating, deformation or surface damage. It can produce a sudden increase in torque and impose abnormal loads on shafts, bearings and couplings.

  • Possible causes: inadequate lubrication, excessive temperature, contamination, misalignment and insufficient clearance.
  • Design considerations: appropriate materials, lubrication, cooling, clearances and bearing selection.
  • Protection: monitoring temperature, vibration and lubrication condition.

8. Blade Release

Blade release is a critical abnormal event in which a blade or blade fragment becomes detached from its attachment. The released mass produces a severe transient load and can damage the casing or neighbouring components.

Design logic: determine blade mass and speed → evaluate centrifugal retention → analyse attachment → consider failure scenario → assess containment and secondary damage.

Blade attachment, disc integrity, casing containment and inspection are therefore major safety considerations.

9. Engine Mountings

Engine mountings transfer engine loads to the supporting structure while maintaining alignment. They must accommodate static loads, dynamic loads, thermal expansion and vibration.

  • Carry engine weight and operational forces.
  • Transmit thrust or reaction loads where applicable.
  • Maintain required alignment.
  • Permit controlled thermal movement.
  • Reduce harmful vibration transmission.

10. Bearings

Bearings support rotating shafts and control their radial and/or axial position while allowing rotation with acceptable friction and heat generation.

RequirementDesign significance
Radial supportControls shaft position under radial loads
Axial locationControls thrust movement
Low frictionReduces power loss and heating
LubricationControls friction, wear and temperature
Dynamic stabilityHelps prevent excessive vibration and instability

11. Major Loads on Rotating Components

  • Centrifugal loading due to rotation.
  • Torque and torsional loading.
  • Aerodynamic or fluid-pressure forces.
  • Thermal stresses caused by temperature gradients.
  • Vibration and dynamic loads.
  • Gyroscopic loads where precession occurs.
  • Transient loads during start-up, shutdown and abnormal events.

12. Major Loads on Pressure Casings

Pressure casings contain pressurised working fluid and protect surrounding structures. Their major loads include internal pressure, thermal gradients, mounting reactions, piping/nozzle forces, vibration and local stresses around openings and joints.

For a simple thin cylindrical pressure vessel: σhoop ≈ pr/t

where p is internal pressure, r is mean radius and t is wall thickness. Real turbine casings require more detailed analysis because geometry, temperature, flanges, openings and constraints make the stress field non-uniform.

13. Load Combination and Design Workflow

1. Define speed, temperature, pressure and operating regime.
2. Identify steady, transient and abnormal load cases.
3. Calculate centrifugal, pressure, torque, bearing and support loads.
4. Determine stresses, deformation and reactions.
5. Check static strength, fatigue, creep and vibration requirements.
6. Check interfaces, attachments, clearances and containment.
7. Select the governing load case and verify safety margin.

CME356 — Unit V: Blade Vibrations and Damage Mechanisms

Official syllabus scope: natural frequencies; blade twist; centrifugal stiffening; excitation; stationary flow disturbance; rotating stall/flutter; Campbell diagram; resonance; temperature, pre-twist and centrifugal stiffening effects; resonance control.

1. Why Blade Vibration Matters

Turbine and compressor blades are flexible structures subjected to periodic aerodynamic forces. If an excitation frequency approaches a blade natural frequency, resonance can occur and vibratory stress can rise sharply.

Excitation frequency ≈ natural frequency → Resonance → High vibration → Fatigue/damage risk

2. Natural Frequency

The natural frequency is the frequency at which a structure tends to vibrate when disturbed and then allowed to vibrate freely.

For a simplified single-degree-of-freedom system:

fn = (1/2π)√(k/m)

Real blades require distributed-mass and structural models. Their natural frequencies depend on geometry, material, boundary condition and rotational speed.

3. Blade Twist

Blade twist is the variation of blade angle along its span. It is often introduced for aerodynamic reasons because the tangential velocity changes with radius.

  • Changes local flow angle.
  • Influences aerodynamic loading.
  • Changes stiffness and mass distribution.
  • Can affect natural frequencies and vibration response.

4. Centrifugal Stiffening

At high rotational speed, centrifugal forces create tensile loading along the blade. This can increase the effective stiffness against certain vibration modes, changing the natural frequencies as speed increases.

Rotational speed ↑ → centrifugal tension ↑ → effective stiffness generally ↑ → natural frequency changes

This is called centrifugal stiffening. The actual frequency shift depends on blade geometry, mode shape and rotational condition.

5. Sources of Excitation

Excitation is a periodic or fluctuating force that drives blade vibration.

  • Stationary vane or nozzle wakes.
  • Rotor-stator interaction.
  • Non-uniform flow.
  • Pressure fluctuations.
  • Manufacturing or assembly variation.
  • Rotating flow disturbances.

6. Stationary Flow Disturbance

Stationary components such as vanes can create repeating disturbances as rotating blades pass them. The resulting excitation frequency is related to rotational speed and the number of stationary flow-disturbing features.

fexc = Nfeatures × frot

This simplified relation helps identify possible harmonic excitation orders.

7. Rotating Stall

Rotating stall is an aerodynamic instability in which stalled flow regions travel circumferentially around a compressor rather than remaining fixed. It can produce strong unsteady loading and blade vibration.

  • Reduces stable operating margin.
  • Produces non-uniform aerodynamic forces.
  • Can increase vibration and fatigue risk.
  • Requires appropriate aerodynamic and control measures.

8. Flutter

Flutter is a self-excited aeroelastic instability in which aerodynamic forces interact with structural vibration. If the aerodynamic energy supplied to the oscillation exceeds the damping losses, vibration can grow.

Structural motion ↔ Unsteady aerodynamic force
Positive energy input → vibration growth → possible flutter

Flutter differs from ordinary forced vibration because the motion itself participates in generating the sustaining aerodynamic excitation.

9. Resonance

Resonance occurs when an excitation frequency is close to a natural frequency. The response can become much larger than the response away from resonance, depending on damping and forcing.

Resonance condition: fexc ≈ fn

In rotating machinery, resonance may occur at specific speed ranges as excitation orders intersect natural-frequency curves.

10. Campbell Diagram

A Campbell diagram plots natural frequencies against rotational speed and overlays excitation-order lines. Intersections identify possible resonance conditions.

Frequency ↑
│ natural-frequency curves
│ /
│ / × ← possible resonance
│/____/____________→ Speed
How to read: identify the operating-speed range → plot/obtain natural-frequency curves → plot excitation orders → locate intersections → evaluate separation and response.

11. Effect of Temperature

Temperature changes material properties and thermal deformation. Changes in elastic modulus, geometry and stress state can shift natural frequencies.

  • Higher temperature can reduce material stiffness in many metals.
  • Thermal expansion changes geometry and clearances.
  • Temperature gradients can create thermal stresses.
  • Temperature-dependent material properties should be used in detailed analysis.

12. Effect of Pre-Twist

Pre-twist changes the blade geometry before operation. It influences aerodynamic performance and structural stiffness and therefore can change modal characteristics.

13. Effect of Centrifugal Stiffening on Modes

Different vibration modes respond differently to rotational stiffening. Therefore, a rotating modal analysis is preferred when accurate resonance prediction is required.

14. Resonance Control

Resonance is controlled by avoiding or reducing harmful frequency coincidence and by limiting vibration response.

  • Modify blade geometry or stiffness.
  • Adjust mass distribution.
  • Change operating-speed range where feasible.
  • Increase damping.
  • Reduce excitation sources.
  • Control clearances and manufacturing variation.
  • Use tuned or friction damping concepts where appropriate.
  • Validate with modal, forced-response and experimental testing.

15. Blade Vibration Design Workflow

1. Establish blade geometry, material and boundary condition.
2. Determine natural frequencies and mode shapes.
3. Include rotational effects and temperature.
4. Identify stationary and rotating excitation orders.
5. Construct/interrogate the Campbell diagram.
6. Check resonance crossings and forced response.
7. Check high-cycle fatigue risk.
8. Modify design or operating conditions and validate.

19. Quick Revision

Natural frequency: structural vibration characteristic.
Excitation: periodic/fluctuating forcing.
Resonance: fexc ≈ fn.
Centrifugal stiffening: rotation-induced tension changes effective stiffness and natural frequency.
Campbell diagram: frequency vs speed with excitation orders.
Control: change frequency, reduce excitation, increase damping, or modify operating range.

1. Wind Energy — Basic Idea

Wind energy is the kinetic energy of moving air converted into useful mechanical or electrical energy by a wind turbine. A wind-energy system mainly consists of the wind resource, rotor, mechanical transmission and generator, together with control, electrical and support systems.

Wind → Rotor blades → Shaft / gearbox → Generator → Electrical power ↑ aerodynamic torque

2. Wind Data

Wind-data collection is required to determine whether a site is suitable for wind-energy conversion. Important data include wind speed, wind direction, duration of observations, turbulence and variation with height.

  • Wind speed: usually measured at a known height using an anemometer.
  • Wind direction: recorded using a wind vane or equivalent instrument.
  • Wind-speed distribution: shows how frequently different wind speeds occur.
  • Measurement height: is important because wind speed generally changes with height above ground.
  • Long-term data: improve confidence in annual energy estimation.

3. Wind Power and Energy Estimation

The power available in an air stream depends strongly on air density, rotor swept area and wind speed. The theoretical kinetic power crossing a rotor area is:

Pwind = 12 ρ A V³

where ρ is air density, A is rotor swept area and V is wind speed.

For a horizontal-axis turbine, the swept area is approximately:

A = πR²

Therefore, wind power is highly sensitive to wind speed because it varies with the cube of V. Actual turbine output is lower than the theoretical wind power because energy remains in the air after the rotor and because of aerodynamic, mechanical and electrical losses.

4. Betz Limit

The Betz limit gives the theoretical maximum fraction of the kinetic power in an ideal steady wind stream that an actuator-disc wind turbine can extract.

Maximum power coefficient: Cp,max = 16/27 ≈ 0.593

Thus an ideal turbine cannot extract all the kinetic energy of the incoming wind. Some air must continue downstream so that mass can flow through the turbine.

For an ideal actuator disc, the maximum power occurs when the axial induction factor is 1/3. In practice, aerodynamic losses, tip losses, mechanical losses and generator losses make the useful coefficient lower than the theoretical limit.

5. Wind-Farm Site Selection

Site selection is a major part of wind-project development. A good site should provide a strong and reasonably consistent wind resource together with suitable terrain, access and grid connection.

6. Wind Resource Assessment

Wind-resource assessment estimates the energy available at a proposed site. Measurements are normally taken over an adequate period and combined with terrain and long-term reference information when required.

  1. Select measurement locations and suitable measurement heights.
  2. Measure wind speed and direction and check data quality.
  3. Determine wind-speed frequency and directional distributions.
  4. Account for terrain, surface roughness and possible wake effects.
  5. Estimate long-term wind conditions and annual energy production.
  6. Evaluate turbine layout, losses and expected project performance.

7. Horizontal-Axis Wind Turbine (HAWT)

A HAWT has its rotor axis approximately parallel to the wind direction. Modern utility-scale turbines commonly use the horizontal-axis configuration.

Blades \ | / \ | / [ ROTOR ] ── Low-speed shaft ── Gearbox ── Generator | Nacelle | Tower | Foundation

Main Components

  • Blades: extract energy from the wind through aerodynamic lift and/or drag.
  • Hub: connects the blades to the main shaft.
  • Main/low-speed shaft: transmits rotor torque.
  • Gearbox: may increase rotational speed for a conventional generator arrangement.
  • Generator: converts mechanical power into electrical power.
  • Nacelle: houses major drivetrain and control components.
  • Yaw system: turns the nacelle to align the rotor with the wind where applicable.
  • Pitch system: changes blade pitch for power and load control in many modern turbines.
  • Brake: provides controlled stopping when required.
  • Tower and foundation: support the turbine and provide elevation above ground.

8. Vertical-Axis Wind Turbine (VAWT)

In a VAWT, the rotor axis is approximately vertical. Its principal advantage is that it can accept wind from different directions without the same type of yaw alignment used by a conventional HAWT.

Common VAWT concepts include lift-based Darrieus-type machines and drag-based Savonius-type machines.

9. Wind-Turbine Generators and Performance

The generator converts shaft power into electrical power. Turbine performance depends on wind speed, rotor aerodynamics, control strategy, drivetrain efficiency, generator efficiency and electrical-system losses.

Power coefficient: Cp = PturbinePwind

The power curve of a turbine relates electrical output to wind speed. Typical operating regions include:

  1. Below cut-in speed: turbine output is essentially zero because available wind is insufficient for useful operation.
  2. Between cut-in and rated speed: output generally increases with wind speed.
  3. Near and above rated speed: control systems limit output around rated power.
  4. Above cut-out speed: the turbine is shut down to protect the machine from excessive loading.

10. Hybrid Wind-Energy Systems

A hybrid renewable-energy system combines wind with one or more complementary sources or storage systems. A common arrangement combines wind, solar PV, batteries and/or a backup generator.

Wind turbine ─┐ ├── Controller / Power electronics ── Load / Grid Solar PV ─────┤ Battery ──────┘

Hybrid systems can reduce the effect of variability when resources complement one another. Design must consider resource availability, load profile, storage size, conversion losses, reliability and cost.

11. Environmental Issues

  • Visual and landscape impact.
  • Noise from aerodynamic and mechanical sources.
  • Bird and bat interactions at some sites.
  • Land use and habitat disturbance during construction and operation.
  • Roads, foundations and transmission infrastructure can affect local ecosystems.
  • End-of-life treatment and material recycling should be considered.

Environmental assessment and appropriate turbine siting can reduce many of these impacts.

12. Applications of Wind Energy

  • Grid-connected electricity generation.
  • Distributed electricity generation.
  • Water pumping.
  • Battery charging in suitable standalone systems.
  • Hybrid renewable-energy systems.
  • Power supply for remote locations when appropriately designed.

1. Energy and Power

Energy is the capacity to do work. Common industrial units include kWh, MJ and GJ.

Power is the rate at which energy is used or transferred.

Power = Energy / Time

Example: a machine that uses the same amount of energy in less time has a higher power requirement.

2. Industrial Energy Scenario

Industries consume energy mainly through electrical systems and thermal systems. Typical energy-intensive areas include motors, pumps, fans, compressors, boilers, furnaces, refrigeration and process heating.

Energy analysis should consider both the quantity of energy consumed and how effectively that energy is converted into useful output.

3. Environmental Aspects of Energy Use

  • Fuel combustion can produce CO₂ and other emissions.
  • Energy generation and transmission can involve losses.
  • Excessive fuel use increases operating cost and environmental impact.
  • Energy conservation reduces unnecessary energy consumption and associated emissions.

4. Material and Energy Balance

A balance compares what enters a system with what leaves or accumulates.

Input = Output + Losses + Accumulation

For a steady-state process, accumulation is zero, so the balance becomes:

Input = Useful output + Losses

In an energy audit, the balance helps locate major energy losses and abnormal consumption.

5. Energy Audit

An energy audit is a systematic study of energy use in a plant to determine where energy is consumed, where losses occur and where economically useful savings are possible.

Need for an energy audit

  • Reduce energy consumption and operating cost.
  • Identify avoidable losses.
  • Improve equipment and process efficiency.
  • Establish energy-performance benchmarks.
  • Prioritize conservation projects.

Types of energy audit

TypeMain purpose
Preliminary auditQuick study using available records and limited measurements to identify major opportunities.
Detailed auditComprehensive measurements, energy balance, loss analysis and technically/economically evaluated recommendations.

6. Energy Audit Methodology

  1. Planning: define scope, objectives, team and data requirements.
  2. Collect records: production data, electricity/fuel bills, operating hours and equipment details.
  3. Walk-through survey: inspect systems and identify obvious losses.
  4. Measurements: measure electrical, thermal and process parameters.
  5. Energy balance: compare inputs, useful output and losses.
  6. Identify opportunities: find avoidable losses and efficiency improvements.
  7. Economic evaluation: estimate investment, savings and payback.
  8. Report: rank recommendations and prepare an implementation plan.
  9. Follow-up: verify actual savings after implementation.

Basic audit sequence

7. Barriers to Energy Conservation

  • Lack of reliable energy data.
  • Limited technical awareness.
  • Insufficient capital for improvement projects.
  • Production priorities may delay shutdown-based improvements.
  • Difficulty proving savings before implementation.
  • Weak follow-up and lack of energy-management ownership.

8. Role of an Energy Manager

  • Monitor and analyse energy consumption.
  • Coordinate energy audits and conservation projects.
  • Maintain energy records and performance indicators.
  • Identify and prioritize energy-saving opportunities.
  • Promote energy-efficient operation and awareness.
  • Track implemented projects and verify savings.

9. Basic Instruments for Energy Auditing

InstrumentTypical use

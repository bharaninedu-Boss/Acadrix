1. Electricity Tariff Structures

An electricity tariff is the method used by the electricity supplier to calculate the amount payable by a consumer. Industrial bills may contain components related to energy consumed, maximum demand and applicable charges.

Common tariff components

  • Fixed / demand charge: based on sanctioned or recorded demand.
  • Energy charge: based on electrical energy consumed, normally in kWh.
  • Power-factor related charge: may be applied as a penalty or incentive according to the tariff.
  • Other applicable charges: taxes, duties and adjustments depending on the tariff.
Energy consumed = Power × Time

2. Typical Industrial Electricity Bill

The important quantities to identify from an industrial bill are connected load, maximum demand, units consumed, power factor and applicable tariff rates.

ItemMeaning
Connected loadTotal rated load connected to the supply.
Maximum demandHighest demand recorded during the billing period according to the metering system.
Energy consumptionElectrical energy consumed during the billing period.
Power factorIndicates how effectively apparent power is converted into useful active power.
Power factor = kW / kVA

3. Demand Side Management (DSM)

Demand Side Management means planning and controlling the consumer's electrical demand so that energy is used efficiently and demand-related costs are reduced.

Main DSM measures

  1. Shift flexible loads away from peak-demand periods.
  2. Improve power factor.
  3. Use energy-efficient motors and equipment.
  4. Switch off idle equipment.
  5. Use automatic controls and suitable operating schedules.
  6. Monitor maximum demand and avoid unnecessary simultaneous operation.
Simple DSM flow
Measure demand → Identify peak loads → Shift/control loads → Reduce peak demand → Review savings

4. HT and LT Supply

LT (Low Tension) supply is used for lower-voltage applications, while HT (High Tension) supply is used where larger industrial loads are supplied at higher voltage levels.

FeatureLT SupplyHT Supply
Typical applicationSmaller loadsLarge industrial loads
Supply voltageLowerHigher
Consumer equipmentUsually simpler metering/supply arrangementRequires suitable transformer, switchgear and protection arrangement

Exam point: Do not write a single universal voltage value unless the question specifies the applicable utility/tariff standard.

5. Power Factor

Power factor is the ratio of active power to apparent power in an AC system. A low power factor causes higher current for the same useful power transfer.

PF = Active power / Apparent power = kW / kVA

Effects of low power factor

  • Higher current for a given kW load.
  • Higher I²R losses in conductors and equipment.
  • Greater voltage drop.
  • Reduced effective capacity of electrical equipment.
  • Possible tariff penalty where applicable.

Power-factor improvement

Capacitor banks are commonly used for improving lagging power factor caused by inductive loads. Automatic power-factor correction can switch capacitor stages according to load conditions.

Power-factor correction
AC supply → Inductive load → Low PF
                     ↘ Capacitor bank → Reactive-power compensation → Improved PF

6. Energy Conservation in Transformers

Transformer losses occur mainly as core losses and winding losses. Conservation aims to reduce unnecessary losses and operate the transformer close to an efficient loading condition.

Important losses

  • Core loss: hysteresis and eddy-current losses.
  • Copper loss: winding I²R loss.
  • Additional losses can occur due to leakage flux and other effects.

Energy-saving measures

  • Select an appropriately rated transformer.
  • Avoid prolonged operation at unsuitable loading.
  • Maintain cooling and ventilation.
  • Keep connections and terminals in good condition.
  • Use energy-efficient transformers where economically justified.
  • Monitor loading, temperature and losses.
Copper loss ∝ I²

7. Harmonics

Harmonics are sinusoidal voltage or current components whose frequencies are integer multiples of the fundamental frequency. Non-linear loads can introduce harmonic currents into an electrical system.

Common sources

  • Variable-frequency drives
  • Rectifiers and power electronic converters
  • Switch-mode power supplies
  • UPS systems
  • Other non-linear industrial loads

Effects

  • Additional heating in transformers and conductors.
  • Increased losses.
  • Distortion of voltage/current waveforms.
  • Possible interference with sensitive equipment.
  • Reduced equipment life when severe.

Reduction methods

  • Harmonic filters.
  • Suitable system design and equipment selection.
  • Low-harmonic or active-front-end drives where appropriate.
  • Proper monitoring of harmonic distortion.
Harmonic concept
Fundamental: f
3rd harmonic: 3f
5th harmonic: 5f
7th harmonic: 7f

Distorted waveform = fundamental + harmonic components

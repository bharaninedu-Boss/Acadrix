CME341 — Unit IV: Component Design — Casting Consideration

R-2021 • 9 Periods • Exam-focused ACADRIX notes

Core idea: design the casting so it can be moulded, removed from the mould, cored, solidified and machined with minimum difficulty and cost.

1. Why Casting Must Be Considered During Design

Casting can produce complex shapes, but the final component must be compatible with the moulding process. A design that looks excellent in CAD may require unnecessary cores, complicated moulds, difficult parting surfaces or excessive machining.

Exam sentence: Design for casting aims to obtain the required shape and function with simple mould construction, minimum cores, easy pattern withdrawal, minimum machining and economical production.

2. Main Casting Design Checks

Parting line

Choose a parting line that permits easy pattern withdrawal and simple mould construction.

Core requirement

Avoid internal cavities that unnecessarily require separate cores.

Machining allowance

Provide machining only where necessary and avoid unnecessarily large machined surfaces.

Section changes

Avoid abrupt changes in section where they can create casting and solidification difficulties.

Draft

Provide suitable draft on surfaces that must be withdrawn from the mould.

Economy

Balance pattern, core, moulding, casting, machining and inspection costs.

3. Redesign Based on Parting-Line Considerations

The parting line is the interface where the mould halves meet. A good parting line should make the pattern easy to withdraw and should avoid unnecessary complicated mould features.

  • Prefer a simple and accessible parting surface.
  • Keep important locating and functional features away from unnecessary parting-line mismatch when possible.
  • Avoid geometry that traps the pattern inside the mould.
  • Reduce the number of mould pieces and special moulding operations.
  • Consider machining and inspection after casting while selecting the parting direction.
Remember: In a long-answer sketch, clearly mark the parting line, pattern withdrawal direction and the major geometry change.

4. Minimizing Core Requirements

A core forms an internal cavity or passage in a casting. Cores add pattern/mould complexity, handling, setting and finishing work. Therefore, the designer should first ask whether the required internal shape can be produced without a core.

  • Eliminate unnecessary internal cavities.
  • Change wall geometry when the same function can be obtained without a core.
  • Use simpler through openings where function permits.
  • Combine cavities or redesign them so a simpler core can be used.
  • When a core is unavoidable, make it easy to locate, support and remove.

5. Machined Holes in Castings

Cast holes can reduce drilling work, but very small, deep, accurate or highly finished holes may still be better produced by machining. The designer must distinguish between holes that can be cast economically and holes that require accurate machining.

Use cast holes when

  • The hole is large enough for practical core formation.
  • Its accuracy and surface finish requirements permit casting.
  • The casting process can reproduce the feature reliably.

Use machined holes when

  • High dimensional accuracy is required.
  • Close positional tolerance is necessary.
  • Fine surface finish is functionally important.
  • A small or difficult-to-cast hole would require excessive core complexity.

6. Redesign of Cast Members to Obviate Cores

The objective is to replace a core-dependent geometry with a shape that can be moulded directly. This is often done by opening a cavity, changing the direction of an opening, splitting a complex feature into simpler surfaces, or changing a hollow shape into a ribbed/open construction.

  1. Identify the internal feature that requires the core.
  2. Ask whether the feature is essential to function.
  3. If essential, search for an alternative geometry.
  4. Check strength, stiffness, weight and assembly after redesign.
  5. Compare casting, machining and assembly cost.

7. Identification of Uneconomical Design

An uneconomical casting is not necessarily one that cannot be manufactured. It is a design that requires unnecessary manufacturing effort, tooling, cores, machining, inspection or material.

Warning signPossible consequenceDesign response
Complex parting lineComplicated moulding and higher tooling effortSimplify geometry and parting direction.
Many coresHigher core-making and setting costRedesign internal cavities.
Large machined surfacesMore machining time and material removalReduce machining allowance/area where function permits.
Unnecessary small featuresDifficult moulding and finishingRemove, enlarge or relocate features.
Many separate casting piecesMore joints and assembly workConsider amalgamation where practical.

8. Modifying the Design

Design modification should not be done only for manufacturing convenience. The redesigned component must still satisfy its functional requirements.

Function

Check load carrying, motion, sealing, heat transfer and other required functions.

Manufacturing

Check moulding, core use, draft, machining and inspection.

Economics

Compare material, tooling, casting, machining, labour and assembly costs.

Quality

Check dimensional accuracy, surface condition, defects and repeatability.

Exam method: Show existing design → manufacturing problem → modified design → benefit. This makes a redesign answer easy to follow.

9. Group Technology

Group Technology (GT) groups similar components into families based on similarities in design and/or manufacturing characteristics. Similar parts can then share manufacturing methods, tooling, fixtures, process plans and knowledge.

  • Identify part families.
  • Classify and code components according to relevant characteristics.
  • Develop common process plans where practical.
  • Use common tooling, fixtures and machines.
  • Reduce setup and planning effort.

10. Computer Applications for DFMA

Computer tools support Design for Manufacture and Assembly by connecting product geometry with manufacturing and assembly considerations. The exact software may vary, but the engineering purpose is to identify problems early in the design stage.

CAD

Creates and modifies 3D product geometry and allows design alternatives to be evaluated.

CAM

Supports manufacturing process planning, tool paths and machining preparation.

DFMA analysis

Helps evaluate part count, assembly sequence, accessibility and manufacturing difficulty.

Digital inspection

Supports dimensional and geometric verification against the intended design.

11. Casting Redesign: Step-by-Step Exam Answer

  1. State the original function. Explain what the component must do.
  2. Identify the casting problem. Mention parting line, core, machining or tooling difficulty.
  3. Draw the original design. Label the troublesome feature.
  4. Propose the redesign. Simplify the mould/core/machining requirement.
  5. Draw the modified design. Mark parting line/core/important features.
  6. Check function. Confirm strength, stiffness, assembly and required geometry.
  7. State benefits. Lower tooling/core/machining effort, easier moulding and better economy where applicable.

13. Two-Mark Quick Revision

1. What is a parting line?

The interface at which mould portions meet and separate for pattern withdrawal.

2. What is a core?

A mould element used to form an internal cavity or passage in a casting.

3. Why minimize cores?

To reduce mould complexity, core-making/setting work and cost.

4. What is a machined hole?

A hole produced or finished by a machining operation to obtain required accuracy or finish.

5. What is an uneconomical casting design?

A design requiring unnecessary tooling, cores, machining, inspection, material or assembly effort.

6. What is Group Technology?

Grouping similar parts into families to use common manufacturing methods and resources.

7. What is DFMA?

Design for Manufacture and Assembly: designing products to simplify manufacturing and assembly.

8. Why use CAD in DFMA?

To create, modify and evaluate product geometry before production.

14. How to Write a 13-Mark Answer

  1. Definition / objective — 1 to 2 marks.
  2. Principle and design considerations — 3 to 4 marks.
  3. Neat labelled diagram(s) — 3 to 4 marks.
  4. Redesign steps / comparison — 2 to 3 marks.
  5. Advantages and economic effect — 1 to 2 marks.

Best presentation: use headings, short points, labelled sketches and a clear Before → Problem → After → Benefit sequence.

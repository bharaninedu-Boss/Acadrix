Problem 1 — Natural Frequency

Given: Equivalent stiffness k = 80,000 N/m; equivalent mass m = 5 kg.
Find: Natural frequency.
Formula: fn = (1/2π)√(k/m)
Substitute: fn = (1/2π)√(80,000/5)
Arithmetic: 80,000/5 = 16,000; √16,000 = 126.49 rad/s.
Final: fn = 126.49/(2π) = 20.13 Hz.

Problem 2 — Stationary Vane Excitation Frequency

Given: Rotor speed N = 6,000 rpm; stationary vanes = 24.
Find: simplified blade-passing excitation frequency.
Step 1 — Convert speed: frot = 6,000/60 = 100 Hz.
Step 2 — Formula: fexc = Nfeatures × frot.
Substitute: fexc = 24 × 100.
Final: fexc = 2,400 Hz.

Problem 3 — Resonance Check

Given: A blade natural frequency is 2,380 Hz at the operating condition. A stationary-flow excitation is 2,400 Hz.
Find: whether the frequencies are close enough to require resonance investigation.
Step 1: Frequency difference = 2,400 − 2,380 = 20 Hz.
Step 2: Relative difference = 20/2,380 × 100 = 0.84%.
Conclusion: The frequencies are very close. This should be treated as a potential resonance condition requiring detailed forced-response/damping analysis; a numerical tolerance for acceptable separation must come from the applicable design criteria rather than being invented here.

Problem 4 — Rotational Speed for a Target Excitation

Given: 30 stationary features; target excitation frequency = 1,500 Hz.
Find: rotor speed corresponding to this excitation order.
Formula: fexc = Nfeatures × N/60.
Rearrange: N = 60fexc/Nfeatures.
Substitute: N = (60 × 1,500)/30.
Arithmetic: N = 90,000/30.
Final: N = 3,000 rpm.

Problem 5 — Frequency Change from an Idealised Stiffness Increase

Given: Simplified blade model has k = 50,000 N/m and m = 2 kg. Rotation changes the effective stiffness to 60,000 N/m while equivalent mass remains 2 kg.
Find: the simplified natural frequencies before and after the stiffness change.
Initial: fn1 = (1/2π)√(50,000/2) = (1/2π)√25,000 = 25.16 Hz.
After stiffening: fn2 = (1/2π)√(60,000/2) = (1/2π)√30,000 = 27.57 Hz.
Change: Δf = 27.57 − 25.16 = 2.41 Hz, approximately 9.6%.
Conclusion: In this idealised model, increased effective stiffness raises natural frequency. Real rotating blades require rotating structural/modal analysis.

Problem 6 — Campbell-Diagram Interpretation

Given: At 4,000 rpm, the 1× rotational excitation is 66.67 Hz. A measured blade natural frequency is 68 Hz.
Find: whether a possible crossing should be investigated.
Step 1: 1× excitation = 4,000/60 = 66.67 Hz.
Step 2: Separation = 68 − 66.67 = 1.33 Hz.
Conclusion: The excitation and natural frequency are close. On a Campbell diagram this point lies near a potential crossing; detailed resonance analysis is required.

Problem 7 — 12-Mark Answer: Campbell Diagram and Resonance

Question: Explain how a Campbell diagram is used to identify possible resonance in rotating blades.
1. Define: Natural frequency is the free-vibration characteristic of the blade; excitation frequency is produced by periodic aerodynamic or mechanical disturbances.
2. Establish operating range: Determine minimum, normal and maximum rotational speeds.
3. Obtain natural frequencies: Determine blade modes using an appropriate structural/modal model, including rotational effects and temperature where required.
4. Determine excitation orders: Convert rotational speed and disturbance-feature counts into excitation frequencies.
5. Plot: Plot frequency vertically and rotational speed horizontally; overlay natural-frequency curves and excitation-order lines.
6. Identify intersections: Intersections indicate possible resonance speeds.
7. Assess response: Evaluate damping, forcing amplitude, mode shape, stress and fatigue risk.
8. Control: Modify stiffness/mass distribution, reduce excitation, increase damping or alter the operating range where feasible.
Conclusion: A Campbell diagram is a screening and design tool for identifying speed ranges requiring detailed vibration and resonance assessment.

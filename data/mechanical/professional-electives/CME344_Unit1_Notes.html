1. Product Life Cycle — Basic Idea

A product does not exist only during manufacturing. It moves through a sequence of activities beginning with an idea or requirement and continuing through design, development, production, use/service and eventual end-of-life. During these stages, large amounts of product information are created and modified.

2. What is PLM?

Product Life Cycle Management (PLM) is an organised approach for managing product information, product-related processes and collaboration throughout the product life cycle.

Why is PLM needed?

  • Many departments create and use the same product information.
  • Product data exists in drawings, CAD models, specifications, bills of materials and other records.
  • Multiple versions and revisions must be distinguished.
  • Engineering changes must be reviewed, approved and communicated.
  • Users need access to the correct product definition at the correct stage.
  • Information must remain traceable from creation through later life-cycle stages.

Opportunities provided by PLM

  • Controlled common product information.
  • Better collaboration between engineering, manufacturing, quality and service functions.
  • Controlled engineering change and revision processes.
  • Reuse of previous product knowledge.
  • Improved traceability and configuration control.
  • Reduction of duplicate and obsolete information.
  • Better visibility of product-development activities.
Remember: PLM is not simply a file-storage system. It connects product information + people + processes + collaboration across the product life cycle.

4. PLM/PDM Infrastructure

PLM/PDM infrastructure is the technical foundation required to create, store, communicate, control and integrate product information.

Main elements

  1. Network: connects users, servers and locations.
  2. Communication: supports exchange of product information between users and applications.
  3. Data management: controls product data, documents, metadata, structures and relationships.
  4. Access control: determines who may view, modify, review, approve or release information.
  5. Application integration: connects PLM/PDM with CAD, CAE, ERP, legacy and other applications.

5. Heterogeneous Data Sources and Applications

Heterogeneous means that information comes from different applications, platforms, formats or structures. A mechanical product can involve CAD models, drawings, BOMs, specifications, analysis results, manufacturing information, quality records and service information.

Problems caused by heterogeneous information

  • Different file formats and data structures.
  • Different naming and identification schemes.
  • Different revision and status conventions.
  • Duplicate information in different systems.
  • Difficulty maintaining consistent product definition.
  • Integration difficulty between old and new applications.

PLM/PDM provides a controlled environment in which related information can be identified, linked, accessed and maintained in its appropriate life-cycle context.

6. PLM Working Principle — Understand Before Writing

The working principle of PLM is best understood as a controlled information and process flow. The system does not merely store a file; it controls the state and use of product information.

The complete cycle can therefore be remembered as:

Creation → Identification → Storage → Access control → Review → Approval → Release → Use → Change → Revision → Feedback

7. Detailed Working of PLM — Stage by Stage

Step 1 — Creation of Product Information

Product information is first created during product development or captured from another activity. Examples include a CAD model, engineering drawing, material specification, product requirement, BOM, test result or technical document.

The important point is that the information becomes part of the controlled product definition rather than remaining as an isolated personal file.

Example: An engineer creates a new CAD model for a gearbox housing.

Step 2 — Identification

The information must be uniquely identifiable so that users can distinguish one item from another. Identification can involve a part number, document number, name, description, metadata and revision/status information.

Good identification prevents confusion between similar components or documents and helps users retrieve the required product information.

Example: The gearbox housing is associated with a unique part number and its drawing is linked to that product definition.

Step 3 — Controlled Storage

The identified information is stored in the PLM/PDM environment together with its relevant relationships and attributes. Storage is not only about keeping the file; it also preserves the context in which the information belongs.

  • Document or CAD file
  • Metadata
  • Product structure relationships
  • Revision/status information
  • Links to related product information

Result: users can retrieve the information as part of the product definition instead of searching through unrelated personal folders.

Step 4 — Access Control

Different users have different responsibilities. PLM/PDM can therefore control access according to roles and permissions.

  • A designer may create or modify engineering information.
  • A reviewer may check the information.
  • An authorised person may approve or release it.
  • Downstream users may use released information without changing the engineering definition.

This reduces the possibility of unauthorised modification or uncontrolled use.

Step 5 — Review and Approval

Before information becomes an approved product definition, it can pass through a workflow. The responsible people review the information against the required technical and organisational criteria.

For example, a drawing may be checked for dimensions, tolerances, material information and completeness before approval.

Important: review and approval establish controlled responsibility. The user should not simply write “the file is checked”; explain that workflow directs the information to the appropriate people for review and approval.

Step 6 — Release

After the required review and approval, the information can be released for its intended downstream use. Release establishes that the particular version has an approved status.

A released drawing can then be used by manufacturing, purchasing, quality or other authorised functions according to their requirements.

Why it matters: production should not accidentally use an unapproved draft when an approved revision is available.

Step 7 — Use Across the Product Life Cycle

Released product information is used by different functions. Engineering may use the design definition; manufacturing may use drawings and product structures; quality may use specifications; service may need product and configuration information.

PLM provides a common controlled context so that downstream activities can refer to the appropriate product information.

Step 8 — Engineering Change

A product may need a change because of a design improvement, manufacturing issue, quality problem, cost consideration, service feedback or another legitimate requirement.

The change should not be made by silently overwriting the released information. A controlled change process is used to identify the reason, evaluate the impact and obtain the required approval.

Example: A gearbox housing requires a modified mounting hole. The proposed change is evaluated before the new definition is released.

Step 9 — Revision and Traceability

After an approved change, a new revision of the product information is established. The previous state remains part of the history rather than simply disappearing.

This allows authorised users to answer questions such as:

  • What was the previous revision?
  • What changed?
  • When was it changed?
  • Why was it changed?
  • Who was responsible for the relevant workflow action?
  • Which revision is currently released?

This historical relationship is the basis of traceability.

Step 10 — Feedback and Further Improvement

Information from manufacturing, testing, service and other later life-cycle activities can identify problems or opportunities for improvement. This feedback can become an input to another controlled change cycle.

Therefore, PLM connects the later stages of the product life cycle back to product development rather than treating development as a one-time activity.

8. How PLM Prevents Outdated Information from Being Used

This is an important part of the 12-mark answer. Explain the control mechanism rather than only saying “PLM avoids old files.”

  1. Unique identification: product information can be distinguished clearly.
  2. Revision control: changed information receives a controlled new revision/state.
  3. Status control: information can be distinguished by its lifecycle status.
  4. Access control: users receive permissions appropriate to their roles.
  5. Workflow: review and approval occur before release.
  6. Traceability: the history of changes can be followed.
  7. Controlled release: downstream functions can use the approved product definition.
  8. Feedback/change cycle: future improvements are introduced through controlled changes instead of uncontrolled overwriting.
Core idea: PLM does not make old information magically disappear. It controls its identification, revision, status, access and release so users can distinguish current approved information from previous or unfinished information.

9. Complete Practical Example — Gearbox Housing Change

Use this example to understand how all the stages connect.

  1. An engineer creates the gearbox housing CAD model and drawing.
  2. The product and document are given controlled identification.
  3. The CAD data and drawing are stored with their related product information.
  4. Access permissions determine who can edit or review them.
  5. The drawing passes through review and approval.
  6. The approved revision is released for downstream use.
  7. Manufacturing uses the released drawing.
  8. During manufacturing, a problem with a mounting hole is identified.
  9. A controlled engineering change is initiated and its impact is evaluated.
  10. The modified drawing is reviewed and approved.
  11. A new revision is released while the previous revision remains traceable as historical information.
  12. Manufacturing receives the current released definition, and the manufacturing experience becomes feedback for further improvement.

Exam conclusion from the example: PLM maintains continuity between product creation, controlled use and subsequent change. This is why PLM is broader than simple document storage.

10. Important 2-Mark Questions & Answers

Q1. Define PLM.

Answer: Product Life Cycle Management is an organised approach for managing product information, product-related processes and collaboration throughout the product life cycle.

Q2. State the need for PLM.

Answer: PLM is needed to control product information, revisions and changes and to provide consistent product information to different functions throughout the product life cycle.

Q3. Expand EDM, PDM, cPDm and CPC.

Answer: EDM — Engineering Data Management; PDM — Product Data Management; cPDm — Collaborative Product Definition Management; CPC — Collaborative Product Commerce.

Q4. What is PDM?

Answer: Product Data Management is the controlled management of product-definition data, documents, versions, structures and related information.

Q5. What is cPDm?

Answer: cPDm means Collaborative Product Definition Management. It focuses on managing product definition while supporting collaboration during product development.

Q6. What is CPC?

Answer: CPC means Collaborative Product Commerce. It represents broader collaboration around product information and product-related activities.

Q7. What is a heterogeneous data source?

Answer: It is a data source that differs from another source in format, structure, application or platform, such as CAD data, ERP records and legacy databases.

Q8. State two opportunities of PLM.

Answer: Improved cross-functional collaboration and better control of product revisions and engineering changes. Information reuse and improved traceability are other opportunities.

Q9. What is PLM/PDM infrastructure?

Answer: It is the combination of network, communication, data-management, access-control and application-integration facilities used to manage product information.

Q10. Why is data management important in PLM?

Answer: It helps product information to be identified, stored, controlled, retrieved and related correctly so that users work with the required product definition.

12-Mark Q1. Explain PLM, its need, opportunities and role throughout the product life cycle.

Introduction

Modern products generate large quantities of information during design, development, production, use and service. Several functions may create and use the same information. PLM provides an organised approach for managing this product information, the related processes and collaboration throughout the product life cycle.

Need for PLM

  1. Large quantity of product information must be controlled.
  2. Many departments require common product information.
  3. Different versions and revisions must be distinguished.
  4. Engineering changes require controlled review and approval.
  5. Product information must remain traceable.
  6. Distributed teams require controlled collaboration and access.

Opportunities of PLM

PLM provides opportunities for controlled information sharing, cross-functional collaboration, engineering-change management, reuse of product knowledge, configuration control, traceability and improved visibility of product-development activities.

Role through the life cycle

Product information is created during development, identified and stored, reviewed and approved, released for use, modified through controlled changes and revised as required. Feedback from later stages can support future improvements.

Conclusion

PLM provides continuity and control across the product life cycle by connecting people, product information and processes in a controlled environment.

12-Mark Q5. Explain the working principle of PLM and its role in controlling product information.

1. Introduction

Product Life Cycle Management is used to control product information and related processes throughout the product life cycle. Its working principle is based on a controlled flow of information from creation through use, change, revision and feedback.

2. Basic PLM information flow

Draw the following flow diagram in the examination:

Creation → Identification → Storage → Access Control → Review → Approval → Release → Use → Change → Revision → Feedback → controlled improvement

3. Creation of information

The cycle begins when product information is created or captured. Examples include CAD models, engineering drawings, specifications, product requirements, BOMs and test information. The information becomes part of the controlled product definition.

4. Identification

Each product item or document must be identifiable. Part numbers, document numbers, descriptions, metadata and revision/status information help users distinguish one item from another and retrieve the required information correctly.

5. Storage and product context

The identified information is stored in the controlled PLM/PDM environment. The important point is that the information can remain associated with its product structure, attributes, relationships and lifecycle state.

6. Access control

Users have different responsibilities. Access control determines who may view, modify, review, approve or release information. This helps prevent unauthorised modification and supports controlled collaboration.

7. Review and approval

Product information can pass through a workflow in which the responsible personnel review it. Required checks are performed before the information is approved. This creates controlled responsibility for the product definition.

8. Release

After approval, the information can be released for intended downstream use. The released definition can then be used by functions such as manufacturing, quality, purchasing or service according to their authorised requirements.

9. Use across the life cycle

Different functions use the released product information. Because the information is controlled, downstream users can refer to the appropriate product definition rather than relying on disconnected personal copies.

10. Change management

Products may require changes because of design improvement, manufacturing problems, quality issues, service feedback or other requirements. The change should be controlled rather than made by silently overwriting the released information. The reason and impact of the change are evaluated and the required approval is obtained.

11. Revision and traceability

An approved change produces a controlled new revision. The previous state remains traceable as historical information. This allows authorised users to determine what changed, which revision is current and how the product definition evolved.

12. Feedback

Manufacturing, testing, service and other later life-cycle activities can generate feedback. Useful feedback can initiate another controlled improvement/change cycle.

13. Role of PLM in preventing outdated information

  1. Unique identification distinguishes product information.
  2. Revision control distinguishes successive versions.
  3. Status control distinguishes drafts, reviewed information and released information.
  4. Access control restricts modification according to responsibility.
  5. Workflow controls review and approval.
  6. Traceability preserves the history of changes.
  7. Controlled release helps downstream users obtain the approved definition.

14. Practical example

Consider a gearbox housing drawing. The designer creates and identifies the drawing, stores it with its product information, sends it through review and approval, and releases it for manufacturing. If manufacturing reports a mounting-hole problem, a controlled change is initiated. After evaluation and approval, a new revision is released. The previous revision remains traceable, while manufacturing receives the current released definition. Feedback from the change can support further improvement.

15. Conclusion

Thus, PLM controls product information by managing its creation, identification, storage, access, approval, release, use, change and revision. This provides traceability and helps prevent uncontrolled use of outdated product information throughout the product life cycle.

How to score well: Do not write only the arrow sequence. For a 12-mark answer, draw the flow diagram, explain every stage in full sentences, add the outdated-information control mechanism, give one practical example and finish with a conclusion.

12. How to Write Any 12-Mark PLM Answer

  1. Start with a precise definition or introduction.
  2. Draw a relevant, labelled diagram whenever the question involves architecture or working.
  3. Use clear numbered subheadings.
  4. Explain each point in complete sentences, not keywords alone.
  5. For working questions, explain the sequence step by step.
  6. Add a practical engineering example when it improves understanding.
  7. Add advantages, limitations or significance when relevant to the question.
  8. End with a short conclusion directly related to the question.

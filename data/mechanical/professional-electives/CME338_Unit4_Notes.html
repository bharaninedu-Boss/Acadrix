Worksheets and Guidelines

Exam-friendly notes for organizing a value engineering study, developing alternatives, preparing proposals and presenting and auditing the study.

1. Preparation of Value Engineering Worksheets

VE worksheets provide a structured record of the study. They help the team move from facts and functions to ideas, evaluation, development and recommendations.

Typical information recorded

  • Project/product identification and study scope
  • Customer or user requirements
  • Existing functions and performance requirements
  • Relevant costs and cost distribution
  • Ideas generated and their sources
  • Comparison and feasibility results
  • Selected alternatives and development data
  • Financial justification and implementation recommendations

Good worksheet practice: use clear function statements, traceable cost figures, stated assumptions and concise evidence.

2. General and Information Phase

The general phase establishes the study: what is being studied, why the study is required, its boundaries, participants, schedule and expected results.

The information phase collects and validates facts before ideas are generated.

Information to collect

  • Product specifications and performance requirements
  • Drawings, process information and materials
  • Customer/user needs
  • Manufacturing and operating conditions
  • Quality, safety and reliability requirements
  • Current cost and major cost drivers
  • Maintenance, energy and life-cycle information where relevant
Exam point: Do not begin with a preferred redesign. First understand the product, its required functions, constraints and meaningful costs.

3. Function Classification, Relationship and Summary

Function classification

Functions can be classified by their role. A basic function is the principal purpose that must be performed; a secondary function supports the basic function or provides additional required features.

Function relationship

Functions are related logically: one function may explain why another is required or how it is accomplished. FAST is one method for representing such relationships.

Function summary

A function summary condenses the important functions identified during analysis so the team can focus on the functions with significant cost, importance or improvement opportunity.

Function statement format: use an active verb followed by a measurable noun, such as “support load”, “transfer torque” or “control temperature”.

4. Meaningful Costs

A meaningful cost is a cost that can help the VE team understand where improvement effort may have significant impact. The emphasis is on costs relevant to the function and the decision being studied.

Examples of cost information

  • Material and purchased-component costs
  • Direct manufacturing or processing costs
  • Tooling and setup costs
  • Assembly and inspection costs
  • Energy, operating and maintenance costs
  • Failure, downtime or quality-related costs where applicable
  • Other relevant life-cycle costs

Costs should be traceable to a reliable source and their basis should be stated.

5. Cost Analysis

Cost analysis examines where money is being spent and relates that spending to required functions. The objective is to identify significant cost areas and potential value-improvement opportunities.

  1. Collect current cost data.
  2. Break total cost into meaningful elements.
  3. Relate costs to functions where practical.
  4. Identify high-cost or low-worth functions.
  5. Investigate alternative ways of achieving required functions.
  6. Compare developed alternatives using relevant technical and economic criteria.
Important: a high-cost component is not automatically a poor-value component. Its required function, performance, quality and worth must also be considered.

6. Idea Listing and Comparison

During the creative phase, alternatives are listed without prematurely rejecting ideas. After sufficient alternatives have been generated, they are compared systematically.

CriterionQuestion
FunctionDoes the idea perform the required function?
PerformanceDoes it meet required output and operating conditions?
CostCan relevant cost be reduced or value improved?
Quality & reliabilityWill required quality and service life be maintained?
SafetyAre safety requirements satisfied?
ImplementationCan the idea realistically be introduced?

7. Feasibility Ranking

Feasibility ranking helps the team prioritize ideas for further development. A practical screening considers technical feasibility as well as economic and implementation factors.

  • Technical feasibility
  • Economic feasibility
  • Manufacturing feasibility
  • Availability of materials, equipment and suppliers
  • Quality and reliability
  • Safety and regulatory constraints
  • Time and implementation risk

A ranking or scoring system may be used, but the criteria and assumptions should be stated clearly. Ranking is a screening aid, not a substitute for engineering judgment.

8. Investigator Phase and Study Summary

In the investigator phase, promising ideas are examined in greater depth. The team develops enough technical and economic evidence to determine whether an idea should be recommended.

Investigation activities

  • Develop the proposed concept or process.
  • Check calculations and performance.
  • Obtain supplier or manufacturing information where needed.
  • Estimate implementation and life-cycle costs.
  • Identify risks, constraints and required changes.
  • Compare the developed proposal with the existing condition.

The study summary records the important findings, selected alternatives, expected benefits, assumptions and implementation requirements.

9. Guidelines for Writing a Value Engineering Proposal

A VE proposal should allow a decision-maker to understand the problem, proposed change, technical justification and expected benefit without ambiguity.

Suggested structure

  1. Problem/current condition: identify what is being studied.
  2. Required function: state what must be accomplished.
  3. Proposed change: describe the alternative clearly.
  4. Technical justification: explain performance, quality and reliability.
  5. Cost comparison: show relevant current and proposed costs.
  6. Life-cycle impact: include operating, maintenance and other relevant effects.
  7. Risks/constraints: state limitations and implementation requirements.
  8. Recommendation: give a clear decision and next action.
Good proposal: factual, concise, traceable and supported by calculations or evidence.

10. Financial Aspects

Financial evaluation determines whether the expected economic benefit justifies the cost and effort required to implement an alternative.

ItemMeaning
Initial investmentEquipment, tooling, design, installation or other implementation expenditure.
Annual savingsExpected recurring reduction in relevant costs.
Additional annual costNew operating, maintenance or other recurring cost caused by the alternative.
Payback periodTime required for cumulative net savings to recover the initial investment.
Life-cycle economic effectTotal relevant financial impact over the study period.

Financial calculations should state assumptions such as study period, discounting method, production volume and expected savings.

11. Life-Cycle Cost Analysis

Life-cycle cost analysis evaluates the economic consequences of an alternative across its useful life, not only at purchase or manufacturing.

Concept: Initial cost ↓ does not necessarily mean total life-cycle cost ↓.

Relevant elements may include acquisition/design, installation, operation, energy, maintenance, repair, downtime, replacement and disposal. Where appropriate, future costs can be converted to present value using an explicitly stated economic method.

VE decision: select an alternative based on the required function, technical suitability and relevant total economic effect—not simply the lowest initial price.

12. Oral Presentation

The oral presentation communicates the VE study to management, customers or other decision-makers.

Recommended flow

  1. State the problem and study objective.
  2. Explain required functions and important findings.
  3. Show the major cost/value opportunity.
  4. Present the alternatives considered.
  5. Explain the selected proposal and technical evidence.
  6. Present financial and life-cycle benefits.
  7. State risks, implementation needs and recommendation.

Use simple tables, diagrams and before/after comparisons where they make the decision easier to understand.

13. Audit

An audit checks what happened after a VE recommendation or study and whether the expected results were achieved.

Audit checks

  • Was the approved change implemented?
  • Was the required function maintained?
  • Were projected savings or benefits achieved?
  • Were quality, reliability and safety maintained?
  • Were implementation costs and assumptions accurate?
  • What lessons should be used in future VE studies?

Purpose: close the loop between recommendation and actual result, improving the effectiveness of future value engineering work.

14. Case Studies and Discussion

Case studies apply the VE process to a real or practice product, process or service. A useful case should identify the required functions, current cost, opportunities, alternatives, evaluation and recommendation.

Case-study workflow

1. Define

Scope, customer requirements and constraints.

2. Analyze

Functions, relationships and meaningful costs.

3. Create

Generate multiple alternatives.

4. Evaluate

Compare technical, economic and implementation factors.

5. Develop

Investigate the best feasible alternative.

6. Recommend

Prepare proposal, presentation and financial justification.

7. Audit

Check actual implementation and benefits.

2-Mark Quick Revision

  • Worksheet: structured record of facts, functions, ideas, evaluation and recommendations in a VE study.
  • Information phase: collection and validation of project, function, cost and requirement data.
  • Meaningful cost: relevant cost information that helps identify important value-improvement opportunities.
  • Feasibility ranking: prioritization of ideas for further development using stated criteria.
  • Investigator phase: detailed development and checking of promising alternatives.
  • VE proposal: documented recommendation explaining the proposed change, justification, cost and benefits.
  • LCC analysis: evaluation of relevant costs over the useful life of an item.
  • VE audit: post-implementation check of actual performance and benefits.

Memory chain: Prepare → Inform → Classify → Cost → Create → Compare → Rank → Investigate → Propose → Finance → Present → Audit.

1. Design for Machining — Basic Idea

A good machining design gives the cutting tool enough access, reduces unnecessary material removal and avoids difficult setups. The designer should consider the machine tool, cutting tool, work-holding method, inspection method and required accuracy before fixing the final geometry.

Exam sentence: A machining-friendly design reduces machining time, tool difficulty, number of setups, material removal and manufacturing cost while maintaining the required function and accuracy.

2. Design Features to Facilitate Machining

Provide tool access

Keep sufficient clearance around the feature so the drill, cutter or tool can approach the surface without interference.

Avoid unnecessary deep features

Deep narrow slots and holes make tool access, chip removal and rigidity more difficult.

Use standard tools

Where practical, design slots, holes and radii so standard cutters and drills can be used.

Reduce setups

Arrange features so several operations can be completed from one datum/setup.

3. Drills

For drilled holes, the designer should consider drill approach, chip evacuation, hole depth, tool length, entry surface and whether the hole must pass completely through the component.

  • Prefer accessible hole locations.
  • Avoid unnecessarily deep, small-diameter holes.
  • Prefer through holes when function permits; they are generally easier to produce and clean.
  • Provide a suitable flat/accessible entry surface.
  • Avoid intersecting holes that create difficult machining unless the intersection is functionally required.

4. Milling Cutters

Milling design should make it possible to use a suitable cutter without excessive overhang. Long slender cutters are less rigid and can deflect or chatter.

  • Keep cutter access clear.
  • Avoid unnecessarily deep narrow pockets.
  • Use practical internal corner radii rather than demanding a perfectly sharp internal corner.
  • Reduce cutter overhang and unnecessary tool reach.
  • Prefer standard cutter sizes where possible.

5. Keyways

Keyways transmit torque between a shaft and hub. From a machining viewpoint, their geometry should allow the selected keyway tool to enter and remove material without unnecessary difficulty.

Good practice

  • Use standard key and keyway dimensions where possible.
  • Provide tool access to the keyway.
  • Avoid unnecessary interrupted or inaccessible machining.

Design check

Consider shaft diameter, hub length, keyway location, cutter access and the assembly method together rather than designing the keyway in isolation.

6. Doweling Procedures

Dowel pins are used to locate parts accurately. The design should provide accessible drilling/reaming locations and a sensible assembly direction.

  • Use dowels primarily for accurate location rather than relying on bolts alone for repeated positioning.
  • Provide access for drilling and reaming.
  • Use a suitable combination of locating and clearance arrangements so the assembly does not become unnecessarily difficult.

7. Countersunk Screws

Countersunk screws allow the screw head to sit flush or below a surface. The countersink must be accessible to the appropriate tool and should not unnecessarily weaken a thin section.

Design check: Do not specify a countersink merely for appearance. Use it when flush seating, clearance or assembly requirements justify it.

8. Reduction of Machined Area

Machining is often expensive because it converts material into chips. A design should avoid machining large surfaces when the same function can be achieved with a smaller machined area.

9. Simplification by Separation

A complicated component can sometimes be divided into simpler parts that are individually easier to manufacture and then assembled.

  • Can simplify machining operations.
  • Can permit standard manufacturing processes for each part.
  • May reduce special tooling.
  • Must be checked against the added assembly, joining and inspection cost.

10. Simplification by Amalgamation

Conversely, separate components can sometimes be combined into one component when this reduces part count and assembly work.

  • Fewer parts and fewer assembly operations.
  • Potentially fewer fasteners and interfaces.
  • May increase manufacturing complexity, so the complete product cost must be evaluated.

Separation

Complex part → simpler parts → assembly

Useful when individual parts become much easier to manufacture.

Amalgamation

Several parts → one integrated part

Useful when part-count and assembly savings outweigh added manufacturing difficulty.

11. Design for Machinability

Machinability means how easily a material/component can be produced by the selected machining process while achieving the required quality and acceptable tool life.

  • Select material and geometry together.
  • Avoid unnecessarily difficult tool paths.
  • Provide adequate rigidity for the workpiece.
  • Use standard tools and practical feature sizes.
  • Reduce unnecessary setups and reorientation.

12. Design for Economy

Economic design considers the total manufacturing cost rather than only material cost.

Cost driverDesign response
MaterialUse material efficiently; avoid excessive stock.
Machining timeReduce unnecessary cutting and machined area.

ToolingPrefer standard tools and avoid special cutters when possible.
InspectionMake critical features accessible for measurement.

13. Design for Clampability

The component should be easy to hold securely during machining. A poor clamping arrangement increases setup time and can cause distortion or unsafe holding.

  • Provide stable surfaces for locating.
  • Avoid clamping on fragile or easily distorted regions.
  • Keep clamps clear of the cutting tool path.
  • Allow quick loading/unloading where production volume justifies it.

14. Design for Accessibility

All manufacturing and inspection features should be physically accessible to the required tool, fixture and measuring instrument.

15. Design for Assembly

Design for assembly (DFA) aims to make assembly simple, reliable and economical. The product should require fewer parts and fewer difficult motions while preventing incorrect assembly.

Manual assembly

  • Easy part handling.
  • Clear orientation.
  • Easy insertion and fastening.
  • Minimize awkward motions.
  • Provide clear access to joints.

Automatic assembly

  • Parts should be easy to feed and orient.
  • Features should guide parts into position.
  • Avoid shapes that jam or nest unpredictably.
  • Keep insertion directions simple.

Robotic assembly

  • Provide predictable gripping surfaces.
  • Maintain repeatable part presentation.
  • Give the robot clear approach paths.
  • Design for sensing and error detection.

19. 13-Mark Answer Structure

  1. Definition / introduction — 1–2 marks.
  2. Design objective — state what manufacturing problem is being avoided.
  3. Neat labelled diagram — show the feature or before/after design.
  4. Main design principles — explain 5–8 logical points.
  5. Example — show how the design change helps.
  6. Benefits — mention machining time, tooling, setup, cost, quality or assembly as relevant.
  7. Conclusion — one or two lines linking the design to manufacturability/economy.

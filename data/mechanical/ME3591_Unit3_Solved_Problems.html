1. Rectangular Fillet-Weld Group

Practice problem matching the ME3591 weld-group design style: a rectangular bar is welded to a support by fillet welds and subjected to an eccentric load.

Given:

Load, P = 25 kN = 25,000 N
Load eccentricity, e = 500 mm
Weld-group dimensions = 150 mm × 100 mm
Permissible weld shear stress, τallow = 75 N/mm²

To find: Size s of the fillet weld.

Step 1 — Convert the load into direct shear

For a rectangular weld group, the total effective weld length is:

L = 2(150 + 100) = 500 mm

Effective throat thickness:

t = 0.707s

Therefore, the total throat area is:

Aw = 0.707s × 500
Aw = 353.5s mm²

Direct shear stress:

τd = P / Aw
τd = 25,000 / (353.5s)
τd = 70.72/s N/mm²

Step 2 — Calculate the bending moment

M = P × e
M = 25,000 × 500
M = 12.5 × 10⁶ N·mm

Step 3 — Calculate bending stress in the weld group

Using the rectangular weld-group section property for the stated 150 mm × 100 mm geometry, the bending stress at the critical weld location can be written in the form:

σb = 785.8/s N/mm²

Step 4 — Combine bending and direct shear

At the critical location, the resultant maximum shear stress is:

τmax = 1/2 √[σb² + 4τd²]
75 = 1/2 √[(785.8/s)² + 4(70.72/s)²]

Square both sides:

150²s² = 785.8² + 4(70.72)²
22,500s² = 617,481.6 + 20,006.5
22,500s² = 637,488.1
s² = 28.333
s = 5.32 mm

Step 5 — Select practical weld size

Calculated weld size is approximately 5.32 mm. Select the next practical size:

Adopt s = 6 mm

Step 6 — Check

Using s = 6 mm:

τd = 70.72/6 = 11.79 N/mm²
σb = 785.8/6 = 130.97 N/mm²
τmax = 1/2 √[(130.97)² + 4(11.79)²]
τmax ≈ 66.56 N/mm²

66.56 N/mm² < 75 N/mm² → Safe.

Result: Adopt a 6 mm fillet weld.

Common mistakes: forgetting the eccentric moment, using weld size s directly as throat thickness, or checking only direct shear.

2. Cotter Joint — Socket-End Dimensions

Practice problem from the ME3591 cotter-joint design pattern.

Given:
Rod diameter, d = 50 mm
Cotter plate thickness, t = 15 mm
Material = 40C8
Syt = 380 N/mm²
Compressive yield strength = 2 × tensile yield strength
Shear yield strength = 50% of tensile yield strength
FOS = 6

To find: Socket-end dimensions.

Step 1 — Calculate allowable stresses

Allowable tensile stress:

σt,allow = Syt / FOS
σt,allow = 380/6 = 63.33 N/mm²

Compressive yield strength:

Syc = 2 × 380 = 760 N/mm²
σc,allow = 760/6 = 126.67 N/mm²

Shear yield strength:

Ssy} = 0.5 × 380 = 190 N/mm²
τallow = 190/6 = 31.67 N/mm²

Step 2 — Calculate the axial load carried by the rod

P = (π/4)d²σt,allow
P = (π/4)(50)²(63.33)
P = 124,348 N

Step 3 — Determine the socket inside diameter

At the socket-end section containing the cotter slot, the remaining material must safely carry the tensile load. Applying the socket-end tensile condition gives the theoretical inside diameter:

d2 ≈ 60.45 mm

Select a practical dimension:

Adopt d2 = 65 mm

Step 4 — Determine socket outside diameter

Check the socket section against tensile and shear failure around the cotter slot using the permissible stresses above. The governing theoretical outside diameter is:

d1 ≈ 84.21 mm
Adopt d1 = 85 mm

Step 5 — Determine socket collar diameter

The collar section is checked against the applicable tensile/shear failure condition. The theoretical collar diameter is:

d3 ≈ 130.44 mm
Adopt d3 = 135 mm

Step 6 — Determine remaining socket dimensions

From the socket-end geometry and the cotter thickness, the calculated values are:

a ≈ 28.04 mm
c ≈ 30.20 mm

Adopt practical dimensions:

a = 30 mm
c = 35 mm

Step 7 — Final dimensional check

The selected dimensions are all greater than the theoretical requirements and should be rechecked using the exact socket/cotter proportions in the prescribed PSG Design Data Book before finalising a drawing.

Result: Adopt d₂ = 65 mm, d₁ = 85 mm, d₃ = 135 mm, a = 30 mm and c = 35 mm.

Common mistake: choosing socket dimensions from a remembered proportion without checking the failure mode. In an examination, write the governing stress equation for each required section.

3. How to solve any Unit III design problem

  1. Draw the joint and label every dimension.
  2. Write the applied load and allowable stresses.
  3. Identify every possible failure mode.
  4. Write one governing equation at a time.
  5. Calculate the theoretical dimension.
  6. Adopt the next practical/standard dimension.
  7. Recalculate the actual stress.
  8. State clearly whether the design is safe.

Standard dimensions should be taken from the permitted PSG Design Data Book. This avoids arbitrary dimensions and keeps the answer aligned with the university examination practice.

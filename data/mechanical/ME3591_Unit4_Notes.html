1. Springs

A spring is an elastic element that stores energy and returns it when the load is removed. Common forms include helical, concentric, laminated and rubber springs.

For a helical spring, important design quantities include wire diameter, mean coil diameter, spring index, number of active coils, stiffness and deflection. Check the spring for allowable stress and required deflection.

Spring index: C = D/d

where D is mean coil diameter and d is wire diameter.

2. Surge in Springs

Surge is a vibration phenomenon in a spring caused when the spring's natural frequency interacts with the forcing frequency. It can produce excessive oscillation and undesirable stress.

Design measures include suitable spring proportions and avoiding operating conditions that strongly excite the spring's natural frequency.

3. Laminated and Rubber Springs

Laminated springs are made from layers of spring material and are widely used where high load capacity and controlled deflection are required. Rubber springs store energy through elastic deformation of rubber and can also provide damping.

4. Flywheels

A flywheel stores rotational energy and reduces speed fluctuation by absorbing excess energy during one part of a cycle and releasing it during another.

For engine flywheels, consider rim stresses and arm stresses. For punching machines, determine the energy required during the working stroke and the permissible speed fluctuation.

5. Connecting Rods

A connecting rod transmits force between the piston and crank mechanism and is subjected to alternating loading. Design requires attention to its cross-section, buckling tendency, inertia loading and strength at critical sections.

6. Crank Shafts

A crankshaft converts reciprocating motion into rotary motion or vice versa. It is subjected to combined bending and torsional loading. Critical sections occur near crank webs, journals and fillets depending on the loading arrangement.

7. 12-Mark Answer — Design of a Helical Compression Spring

  1. State the required load and deflection.
  2. Select spring material and allowable stress from the permitted design data.
  3. Choose a suitable spring index.
  4. Determine wire diameter from the governing stress condition.
  5. Determine mean coil diameter from the spring index.
  6. Determine active coils from the required deflection/stiffness relation.
  7. Determine total coils and free length according to the spring end condition.
  8. Check shear stress, deflection, solid length and stability where applicable.
  9. Check surge/natural-frequency requirements where specified.
  10. Adopt practical standard dimensions and re-check.

Conclusion: A spring must satisfy both strength and functional deflection requirements, with surge avoided where dynamic operation is involved.

8. 12-Mark Answer — Flywheel Design

  1. Determine the required work/energy fluctuation during the cycle.
  2. Determine allowable speed fluctuation.
  3. Calculate the required coefficient of fluctuation of speed.
  4. Determine the required maximum fluctuation of energy.
  5. Relate the energy storage requirement to the flywheel mass and radius.
  6. Select rim dimensions and determine rim stresses.
  7. Check arm stresses and the connection between rim and hub.
  8. For punching-machine service, account for the energy needed during the working stroke.
  9. Check the final design for strength, mass and operating speed.

Conclusion: Flywheel design is governed by energy-storage requirement, allowable speed fluctuation and stresses in the rotating structure.

9. 12-Mark Answer — Connecting Rod Design

  1. Determine maximum gas/inertia loading from the given engine data.
  2. Select material and allowable stress.
  3. Choose a suitable connecting-rod section.
  4. Evaluate compressive/tensile loading and bending effects.
  5. Check the rod for buckling using the applicable design procedure.
  6. Design/check small end and big end regions.
  7. Check critical sections and bearing interfaces.
  8. Adopt practical dimensions and re-check the governing condition.

Conclusion: Connecting-rod design must safely withstand alternating axial loading and the associated bending/buckling effects.

10. 12-Mark Answer — Crankshaft Design

  1. Draw the crankshaft and identify crank-pin, main-journal and crank-web locations.
  2. Determine gas and inertia forces from the given data.
  3. Find bearing reactions.
  4. Calculate bending moments and torque at critical sections.
  5. Design the shaft/journal diameter using the specified combined-loading criterion.
  6. Check torsional and bending stresses.
  7. Check fillets, stress concentration and fatigue considerations where required.
  8. Check crank-web strength and practical proportions.
  9. Adopt standard dimensions and re-check.

Conclusion: A crankshaft is a combined-loading component and must be checked at its critical sections.

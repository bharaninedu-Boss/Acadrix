1. Introduction to Machine Design

Machine design is the systematic process of deciding the material, shape, dimensions and construction of a machine element so that it performs its required function safely and economically.

Basic design sequence

  1. Identify the function and operating requirements.
  2. Determine loads, speed, temperature and working environment.
  3. Select a suitable material.
  4. Determine the possible modes of failure.
  5. Choose an appropriate failure theory and factor of safety.
  6. Calculate the required dimensions from strength and/or stiffness.
  7. Check manufacturability, standard sizes, assembly and maintenance.
  8. Adopt standard dimensions wherever possible and prepare the final design.

2. Factors Influencing Machine Design

  • Type and magnitude of load
  • Static, fluctuating, impact or shock loading
  • Required strength and stiffness
  • Material properties and availability
  • Wear, corrosion and temperature
  • Manufacturing process and tolerances
  • Standardisation and interchangeability
  • Safety, reliability and service life
  • Space, weight and appearance
  • Cost, maintenance and ease of assembly

3. Selection of Materials

Material selection should be based on the mechanical properties required by the component and its service conditions.

4. Preferred Numbers

Preferred numbers are a standardised series of numerical values used to reduce unnecessary variety in component sizes and products. They support standardisation, interchangeability and economical manufacture.

Exam point: When a calculated dimension is obtained, select a suitable standard/preferred size rather than retaining an unnecessarily precise non-standard dimension.

5. Direct, Bending and Torsional Loading

Direct normal stress: σ = P/A
Direct shear stress: τ = P/A
Bending stress: σ = M/Z
Torsional shear stress: τ = T/Zp

For a solid circular shaft, Zp = πd³/16 and bending section modulus Z = πd³/32.

6. Modes of Failure and Factor of Safety

Common failure modes include yielding, fracture, excessive deflection, fatigue failure, buckling, wear and excessive temperature.

FOS = Failure stress / Allowable stress

The allowable stress is selected below the relevant limiting strength according to the design condition and specified factor of safety.

7. Combined Loading and Principal Stresses

Machine members may experience normal stress and shear stress simultaneously. The resulting stress state should be transformed into principal stresses before applying a suitable failure theory.

σ1, σ2 = (σx + σy)/2 ± √[((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²]
τmax = (σ1 − σ2)/2

For a common case of plane stress with σy = 0, the relation can be simplified accordingly.

8. Eccentric Loading

An eccentric load does not pass through the centroidal axis. It produces both direct loading and a moment.

M = P × e

The resulting stress is obtained by combining the direct stress and bending stress with the correct sign at the critical location.

9. Curved Beams – Crane Hook and C-Frame

In a curved beam, the neutral axis does not generally coincide with the centroidal axis. Stress distribution is therefore different from the simple straight-beam equation σ = My/I.

Typical applications in this unit include crane hooks and C-frames. The design must identify the critical section and evaluate the curved-beam stress distribution using the prescribed design relation/data book.

Exam focus: Know the difference between straight-beam bending and curved-beam bending, and identify why the neutral axis shifts in a curved member.

10. Theories of Failure

Failure theories are used to predict yielding or failure when a component is subjected to combined stresses.

  • Maximum principal stress theory: compares the maximum principal stress with the limiting normal stress.
  • Maximum shear stress theory (Tresca): compares maximum shear stress with the allowable shear stress.
  • Maximum principal strain theory: considers principal strains.
  • Total strain energy theory: compares total elastic strain energy.
  • Distortion energy theory (von Mises): uses the distortion-energy criterion and is widely used for ductile materials.
Important: In numerical problems, use the failure theory explicitly stated in the question. Do not silently replace it with another theory.

11. Design Based on Strength and Stiffness

Strength design ensures that stresses remain within allowable limits.

Stiffness design ensures that deformation, deflection or twist remains within the permitted value.

A component can be strong enough but still fail its functional requirement because of excessive deflection. Therefore both checks may be necessary.

12. Stress Concentration

Sudden changes in geometry such as holes, keyways, grooves, shoulders and sharp corners cause local stress intensification.

Kt = Maximum local stress / Nominal stress

For fatigue loading, notch sensitivity is used with the theoretical stress-concentration factor to obtain an appropriate fatigue stress-concentration factor.

13. Fluctuating Stresses and Endurance Limit

When stress varies with time, it can be described using maximum stress, minimum stress, mean stress and alternating stress.

σm = (σmax + σmin)/2
σa = (σmax − σmin)/2
Stress ratio R = σmin/σmax

The endurance limit is the stress amplitude associated with the specified fatigue-life criterion. In design, the endurance strength is modified using the relevant factors such as surface condition, size, reliability and other applicable factors.

14. Finite and Infinite Life Design

Infinite-life design: the design is intended to remain below the appropriate fatigue limit for the specified material and conditions.

Finite-life design: the component is designed for a specified number of cycles using the applicable fatigue-life relation or diagram.

Remember: Fatigue design depends on the loading history, mean/alternating stress, material properties, surface and size effects, reliability and the required life.

15. Standards in Design

Standards reduce variety and improve interchangeability, manufacture, inspection and maintenance. In university design problems, use the permitted PSG Design Data Book for standard dimensions, material data, preferred sizes and relevant design charts/tables.

Numerical Problem Workflow

  1. Write Given and Find.
  2. Draw the loading diagram if geometry is involved.
  3. Convert all quantities to consistent units.
  4. Calculate the relevant direct, bending or torsional stresses.
  5. Find principal stresses if required.
  6. Apply the specified failure theory and FOS.
  7. Check strength and stiffness requirements.
  8. Select the nearest suitable PSG standard dimension.
  9. Recalculate/check the adopted dimension.
  10. Box the final answer with units.

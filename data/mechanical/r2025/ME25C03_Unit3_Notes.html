Unit III — Energy Interactions

ME25C03 · R-2025 · Semester 1 · Exam-oriented notes

1 Mark3 Marks12 Marks

Syllabus: system and energy transfer; conduction, convection and radiation; heat engines; refrigeration and HVAC systems.

1. Energy Interactions — Basic Idea

Mechanical engineering systems continuously exchange energy with their surroundings. Energy may cross a system boundary as heat or work, while mass may also carry energy in an open system. Understanding these interactions is essential for engines, refrigerators, compressors, heat exchangers and many thermal systems.

2. Thermodynamic System

A thermodynamic system is a specified quantity of matter or a region in space selected for study. Everything outside it is the surroundings, and the boundary separates the system from its surroundings.

TypeMeaningExample
Closed systemMass does not cross the boundary; energy may cross.Gas in a sealed piston-cylinder.
Open systemMass and energy can cross the boundary.Turbine, compressor or pump.
Isolated systemNeither mass nor energy crosses the boundary ideally.Idealised perfectly insulated sealed system.

System boundary

The boundary may be fixed or movable and may be real or imaginary. The choice of boundary determines which energy interactions need to be considered.

3. Energy and Modes of Transfer

Energy is the capacity to produce change or do work. In thermal engineering, two fundamental ways of transferring energy across a system boundary are heat and work.

InteractionSimple meaning
HeatEnergy transfer caused by a temperature difference.
WorkEnergy transfer associated with a generalised force/displacement interaction other than heat.
Mass transferIn an open system, flowing mass carries energy across the boundary.

Heat is not “stored” in a body as a thermodynamic property; it describes energy in transit because of a temperature difference.

4. Heat Transfer

Heat transfer is the movement of thermal energy caused by a temperature difference. It occurs through three basic modes: conduction, convection and radiation.

5. Conduction

Conduction is heat transfer through a material or between contacting parts of a material due to microscopic interactions. It does not require bulk motion of the material.

Fourier's law, one-dimensional form:
Q̇=−kA(dT/dx)
  • Q̇ is heat-transfer rate.
  • k is thermal conductivity.
  • A is heat-transfer area.
  • The negative sign indicates heat flows in the direction of decreasing temperature.

Examples: heat flow through a metal rod, wall or machine component.

6. Convection

Convection involves heat transfer between a surface and a moving fluid. It combines energy transfer at the surface with fluid motion.

Newton's law of cooling:
Q̇=hA(Tₛ−T∞)

Here h is the convection heat-transfer coefficient, Tₛ is surface temperature and T∞ represents the fluid temperature away from the surface.

Convection may be natural/free convection, where fluid motion arises from buoyancy, or forced convection, where a fan, pump or other device drives the flow.

7. Radiation

Thermal radiation is energy emitted by matter because of its temperature. Unlike conduction and convection, radiation does not require a material medium and can occur through a vacuum.

Idealised blackbody relation:
E_b=σT⁴

Real surfaces have emissivity less than or equal to one. For many introductory comparisons, the important idea is that radiative emission depends strongly on absolute temperature.

Modes compared

ModeNeeds material medium?Main mechanism
ConductionYesMicroscopic interaction within matter.
ConvectionYesSurface-fluid interaction with fluid motion.
RadiationNoElectromagnetic emission/absorption.

8. Heat Engine

A heat engine is a device that operates cyclically and converts part of the energy supplied as heat into useful work while rejecting the remaining heat to a lower-temperature sink.

W_net=Q_H−Q_L
η=W_net/Q_H=1−Q_L/Q_H

Here QH is heat supplied from the high-temperature source, QL is heat rejected to the low-temperature sink and Wnet is net work output.

Simple heat-engine sequence

High-temperature source → heat supplied → engine → net work output + heat rejected → low-temperature sink.

9. Refrigeration

A refrigerator uses work input to transfer heat from a low-temperature region to a higher-temperature surrounding. This is opposite to the natural direction of heat flow and therefore requires external work.

COP_R=Q_L/W_net

A higher coefficient of performance means more cooling effect is obtained per unit of work input under the stated conditions.

10. Heat Pump

A heat pump has the same basic thermodynamic principle as a refrigerator but the useful effect is the heat delivered to the high-temperature space.

COP_HP=Q_H/W_net

For the same cycle, COP_HP=COP_R+1.

11. Refrigeration Cycle — Basic Vapour-Compression Idea

A common refrigeration system uses a compressor, condenser, expansion device and evaporator.

  1. Compressor: raises the pressure of the refrigerant vapour and requires work input.
  2. Condenser: rejects heat to the surroundings and changes high-pressure refrigerant vapour toward the liquid state.
  3. Expansion device: reduces refrigerant pressure.
  4. Evaporator: absorbs heat from the refrigerated space.
Memory sequence: Compressor → Condenser → Expansion device → Evaporator → Compressor.

12. HVAC Systems

HVAC means Heating, Ventilation and Air Conditioning. An HVAC system controls indoor environmental conditions to provide comfort or suitable conditions for a process.

Common functions

  • Temperature control.
  • Humidity control.
  • Ventilation and fresh-air supply.
  • Air filtration/cleaning.
  • Air circulation and distribution.

Comfort-conditioning idea

A comfortable indoor environment depends on temperature, humidity, air movement, air quality and the activity/clothing of occupants. HVAC design balances these requirements with energy efficiency.

13. Engineering Applications

  • IC-engine cooling and thermal management.
  • Refrigerators and cold-storage systems.
  • Building air-conditioning.
  • Electronic and battery thermal management.
  • Heat exchangers and industrial process heating/cooling.
  • Insulation of buildings, pipes and thermal equipment.

18. Quick Revision

Q̇=hA(Tₛ−T∞) — Newton cooling relation
E_b=σT⁴ — ideal blackbody emission relation
W_net=Q_H−Q_L — heat engine energy balance
η=1−Q_L/Q_H — heat-engine efficiency
COP_R=Q_L/W_net — refrigerator COP
COP_HP=Q_H/W_net=COP_R+1 — heat-pump COP

19. Exam Tips

  • Always draw a simple energy-flow diagram for heat-engine and refrigeration questions.
  • Remember that heat flows naturally from higher to lower temperature; refrigeration uses work to move heat from low to high temperature.
  • Do not confuse efficiency with COP.
  • Use absolute temperature when a radiative temperature relation such as T⁴ is used.
  • In conduction/convection numericals, check whether the requested quantity is heat rate or total heat.
  • For HVAC answers, write both comfort functions and ventilation/air-quality functions.

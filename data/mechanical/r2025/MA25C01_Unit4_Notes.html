Unit IV — Multiple Integrals

MA25C01 · R-2025 · Detailed exam-oriented notes

1 Mark3 Marks12 Marks

Syllabus: iterated integrals/Fubini, double integrals, change of order, Cartesian and polar coordinates, triple integrals, and Cartesian/cylindrical/spherical coordinate systems.

Study approach: Understand the region → choose the order/coordinates → write limits → include the Jacobian → evaluate → check the result.

1. Multiple Integrals — Basic Idea

A single integral accumulates a quantity along a one-dimensional interval. A double integral accumulates a quantity over a two-dimensional region, while a triple integral accumulates a quantity throughout a three-dimensional solid.

IntegralRegionTypical meaning when integrand = 1
∫f(x)dxIntervalAccumulated quantity
∬Rf(x,y)dAArea RArea of R
∭Vf(x,y,z)dVVolume VVolume of V

In applications, the integrand need not be 1. For example, a surface quantity can be accumulated over an area, or a density can be accumulated over a volume to obtain total mass.

2. Iterated Integrals and Fubini's Theorem

An iterated integral evaluates a multiple integral through successive single integrations. For a suitable function over a suitable region, Fubini's theorem allows the double integral to be expressed as an iterated integral.

Rectangular region: If a≤x≤b and c≤y≤d, then ∬Rf(x,y)dA=∫ₐᵇ∫𝑐ᵈf(x,y)dy dx. An equivalent order may be used when applicable.

Reading the notation: in dy dx, integrate y first and x second. In dx dy, integrate x first and y second.

Simple example: For ∫₀¹∫₀²(x+y)dy dx, integrate with respect to y first, treating x as constant, then integrate the result with respect to x.

3. Double Integrals

A double integral ∬Rf(x,y)dA represents accumulated values over an area. If f(x,y)≥0, it can be interpreted geometrically as the volume under the surface z=f(x,y) and above R.

Standard rectangular procedure

1. Identify the x- and y-ranges.
2. Decide the order of integration.
3. Write the inner integral and integrate it.
4. Use the result in the outer integral.
5. Simplify and check the answer.

4. Changing the Order of Integration

Changing the order means describing the same geometric region using a different sequence of integration. The limits generally change because the boundaries seen by the inner variable change.

Reliable method

Step 1: Read the original limits and identify the region.
Step 2: Sketch the region and mark all boundary curves.
Step 3: Choose the new inner variable.
Step 4: Determine its lower and upper boundaries.
Step 5: Determine the outer-variable range.
Step 6: Rewrite the integral and evaluate.
Important: Never simply swap dx and dy. The limits describe the geometry and must be reconstructed.

5. Cartesian Coordinates

Cartesian coordinates use perpendicular axes and are generally convenient for rectangles, boxes, straight-line boundaries and regions naturally expressed using x, y and z.

Area: dA=dx dy or dy dx
Volume: dV=dx dy dz in the chosen order.

6. Polar Coordinates

Polar coordinates are useful for planar regions involving circles, disks, sectors and expressions containing x²+y².

x=r cosθ
y=r sinθ
x²+y²=r²
dA=r dr dθ

The factor r is the Jacobian of the transformation. It accounts for the change in area when rectangular coordinates are replaced by polar coordinates.

6.1 Choosing polar limits

  • Determine the angular range from the rays or symmetry.
  • For each θ, determine the radial distance from the origin to the boundary.
  • Use the actual geometry rather than assuming standard limits.
RegionTypical limits
Full disk radius a0≤r≤a, 0≤θ≤2π
First-quadrant sector0≤θ≤π/2, with r determined by boundary
SemicircleUse the corresponding half-plane angular range

6.2 Polar-coordinate workflow

Convert x and y boundaries into r and θ.
Determine θ-limits first.
Determine r-limits for each angle.
Convert the integrand if necessary.
Replace dA by r dr dθ.
Evaluate and check the physical/geometric result.
Example idea: For a full disk, symmetry makes polar coordinates natural. A quantity depending on x²+y² becomes a function of r², and the circular boundary becomes the simple limit 0≤r≤a.

7. Triple Integrals

A triple integral is written ∭Vf(x,y,z)dV. It accumulates a quantity throughout a volume.

  • If f=1, the result is the volume of V.
  • If f represents a volume density, the integral can represent total mass.
  • The order of integration must be consistent with the three-dimensional limits.

Triple-integral workflow

Identify and sketch the solid where possible.
Choose the coordinate system.
Determine the innermost, middle and outer limits.
Write the correct volume element.
Evaluate from inside outward.

8. Cylindrical Coordinates

Cylindrical coordinates extend polar coordinates into three dimensions and are particularly useful for cylinders and solids with circular symmetry around the z-axis.

x=r cosθ
y=r sinθ
z=z
dV=r dr dθ dz
x²+y²=r²

Here r is the distance from the z-axis and θ specifies the direction around that axis. The z-coordinate remains unchanged.

Typical cylindrical geometry

  • Vertical cylinder: radial boundary becomes a simple r-limit.
  • Full rotation: θ often runs through 0≤θ≤2π.
  • Horizontal or vertical planes provide z-limits.

9. Spherical Coordinates

Spherical coordinates are suited to spheres, spherical shells and solids whose boundaries are naturally described by distance from the origin.

x=ρ sinφ cosθ
y=ρ sinφ sinθ
z=ρ cosφ
dV=ρ²sinφ dρ dφ dθ

ρ is radial distance from the origin, θ is the azimuthal angle in the xy-plane, and φ is measured from the positive z-axis.

Full sphere of radius a

0≤ρ≤a
0≤φ≤π
0≤θ≤2π

The Jacobian factor ρ²sinφ is essential.

11. How to Choose the Coordinate System

Ask 1: Are the boundaries mainly straight and aligned with x, y or z? → Cartesian.
Ask 2: Is the planar region circular or does it contain x²+y²? → Polar.
Ask 3: Is the 3D solid cylindrical or symmetric about the z-axis? → Cylindrical.
Ask 4: Is the solid spherical or based on distance from the origin? → Spherical.

The best coordinate system is usually the one that makes the boundary and Jacobian simplest.

15. Worked-Method Examples

Example A — Rectangular double integral

For an integral such as ∫₀ᵇ∫₀ᵃ f(x,y)dy dx, the inner integral is with respect to y. Treat x as constant, evaluate the inner result, substitute the y-limits, then integrate the resulting function with respect to x from 0 to b.

Example B — Polar disk

For a disk x²+y²≤a², use 0≤r≤a and 0≤θ≤2π. Replace x²+y² by r² and dA by r dr dθ. The integral then separates naturally when the transformed integrand permits it.

Example C — Spherical volume

For a full sphere ρ≤a, use 0≤ρ≤a, 0≤φ≤π, 0≤θ≤2π, together with dV=ρ²sinφ dρ dφ dθ. If the integrand is 1, the result is the sphere's volume.

16. 12-Mark Answer Framework

  1. Given: Write the function and region/solid.
  2. Sketch: Draw or clearly describe the region whenever limits are not immediately obvious.
  3. Choose coordinates: Explain briefly why Cartesian/polar/cylindrical/spherical coordinates are suitable.
  4. Set limits: Derive every limit from the geometry.
  5. Jacobian: Include the correct transformation factor.
  6. Integral: Write the complete integral before starting calculation.
  7. Evaluation: Work from the innermost integral outward.
  8. Conclusion: Box the answer and state units or physical meaning when relevant.

17. Common Exam Mistakes

  • Writing limits that do not cover the complete region.
  • Changing integration order without redrawing/reinterpreting the region.
  • Forgetting the polar Jacobian r.
  • Forgetting the cylindrical Jacobian r.
  • Forgetting the spherical Jacobian ρ²sinφ.
  • Using an incorrect angle range.
  • Mixing up spherical φ and θ conventions. Follow the convention used in the problem/notes.
  • Giving only the final answer in a long-answer problem without showing the setup.

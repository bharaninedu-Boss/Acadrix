Unit IV — Multiple Integrals

MA25C01 · R-2025 · Exam-oriented notes

1 Mark3 Marks12 Marks

Syllabus: iterated integrals/Fubini, double integrals, change of order, Cartesian and polar coordinates, triple integrals, and Cartesian/cylindrical/spherical coordinate systems.

How to study this unit: First understand the region → choose the order/coordinates → write limits → include the Jacobian when required → evaluate → check the result.

1. Multiple Integrals — Basic Idea

A single integral accumulates a quantity along an interval. A double integral accumulates a quantity over an area, while a triple integral accumulates a quantity throughout a volume. If the integrand is 1, the double integral gives area and the triple integral gives volume.

Key points

  • dA represents a small area element.
  • dV represents a small volume element.
  • The limits must describe the complete region without missing or duplicating any part.
  • The order of integration determines which variable is integrated first.

2. Iterated Integrals and Fubini's Theorem

For a suitable function over a suitable region, a double integral can be evaluated through successive single integrations. This is called an iterated integral.

Rectangular region: If a≤x≤b and c≤y≤d, then ∫∫Rf(x,y)dA=∫ₐᵇ∫𝑐ᵈf(x,y)dy dx. The other order can also be used when applicable.

Exam point

In dy dx, integrate with respect to y first and x second. In dx dy, integrate with respect to x first and y second.

3. Double Integrals

A double integral of f(x,y) over a region R is written as ∫∫Rf(x,y)dA. Geometrically, when f(x,y)≥0, it represents the volume between the surface z=f(x,y) and the xy-plane over R.

Standard rectangular procedure

  1. Identify the x- and y-ranges.
  2. Write the double integral in an appropriate order.
  3. Integrate the inner integral.
  4. Use the result in the outer integral.
  5. Simplify and state the final answer.

4. Changing the Order of Integration

Changing the order means describing the same region using the other integration sequence. The numerical value does not change when the conditions for changing the order are satisfied.

Reliable method: 1) Read the given limits. 2) Identify the boundary curves/lines. 3) Sketch the region. 4) Decide the new inner variable. 5) Find its lower and upper boundaries. 6) Find the outer-variable limits. 7) Rewrite and evaluate.

Common mistake: Never simply interchange dx and dy without changing the limits. The limits describe the geometry and must be reconstructed.

5. Cartesian Coordinates

Cartesian coordinates use perpendicular x, y and z axes. They are usually the easiest choice when boundaries are straight lines, rectangles, boxes or simple functions.

DimensionElement
PlanedA=dx dy or dy dx
SpacedV=dx dy dz in a chosen order

6. Polar Coordinates

Polar coordinates are useful when the planar region contains circles, disks, sectors or expressions involving x²+y².

x=r cosθ
y=r sinθ
x²+y²=r²
dA=r dr dθ

The extra factor r is the Jacobian of the transformation. Omitting it gives an incorrect integral.

Typical limits

  • Full circle of radius a: 0≤r≤a, 0≤θ≤2π.
  • Semicircle: choose the corresponding half-range for θ.
  • Sector: determine the two bounding angles first.

Polar-coordinate procedure

  1. Convert the boundary into r and θ.
  2. Determine the angular limits.
  3. Determine the radial limits.
  4. Replace dA by r dr dθ.
  5. Convert the integrand if necessary.
  6. Evaluate.

7. Triple Integrals

A triple integral is written as ∭Vf(x,y,z)dV. It accumulates a function throughout a three-dimensional solid.

  • If f=1, the result is the volume of V.
  • For density functions, integration can give total mass when the density is integrated over the volume.
  • The limits must describe the entire solid in the selected order.

8. Cylindrical Coordinates

Cylindrical coordinates extend polar coordinates by retaining the z-coordinate. They are particularly convenient for cylinders and solids with circular symmetry about the z-axis.

x=r cosθ
y=r sinθ
z=z
dV=r dr dθ dz

Also, x²+y²=r², which makes circular cylindrical boundaries much simpler.

9. Spherical Coordinates

Spherical coordinates are useful for spheres and solids whose boundaries naturally involve distance from the origin.

x=ρ sinφ cosθ
y=ρ sinφ sinθ
z=ρ cosφ
dV=ρ²sinφ dρ dφ dθ

Here ρ is the radial distance, θ is the azimuthal angle in the xy-plane, and φ is measured from the positive z-axis.

Full sphere

For a sphere of radius a centred at the origin, typical limits are 0≤ρ≤a, 0≤φ≤π, and 0≤θ≤2π.

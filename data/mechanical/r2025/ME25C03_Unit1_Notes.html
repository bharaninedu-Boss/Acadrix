1. What is Mechanical Engineering?

Mechanical Engineering is the engineering discipline concerned with the design, analysis, manufacture, operation and maintenance of machines, devices and energy systems. It combines mechanics, materials, thermal sciences, manufacturing, mathematics, computing and design.

Why it is important

  • Converts engineering ideas into useful physical products and systems.
  • Supports transportation, manufacturing, energy, automation, healthcare and many other industries.
  • Uses scientific principles to improve safety, efficiency, reliability and sustainability.
  • Provides the foundation for later subjects such as thermodynamics, strength of materials, manufacturing and machine design.

2. History and Evolution of Mechanical Engineering

Mechanical engineering developed from practical mechanical inventions into a modern discipline based on scientific analysis and computer-supported design.

Early engineering: Simple machines such as levers, wheels, pulleys and inclined planes enabled mechanical advantage and useful work.
Industrial Revolution: Steam power, machine tools and mechanised production accelerated the development of systematic mechanical engineering.
20th century: Internal-combustion engines, turbines, automobiles, aircraft and advanced manufacturing expanded the field.
Modern era: CAD/CAE, CNC, robotics, additive manufacturing, mechatronics, smart manufacturing and sustainable energy systems have become important.
Exam point: In a history answer, show the progression from simple machines → steam/mechanisation → engines and mass production → automation and digital/sustainable engineering.

3. Force

Force is an interaction that can change the motion of a body or deform it. It is a vector quantity, so it has both magnitude and direction.

SI unit: newton (N).
1 N = 1 kg·m/s²

Effects of force

  • Can accelerate or decelerate a body.
  • Can change the direction of motion.
  • Can produce rotation when it creates a moment about a point.
  • Can deform a component.

Newton's second law

ΣF=ma

The net force acting on a body equals its mass multiplied by acceleration.

4. Motion

Motion is the change of position of a body with time relative to a reference frame.

QuantityMeaningTypical SI unit
DistanceTotal path lengthm
DisplacementDirected change in positionm

VelocityRate of change of displacementm/s
AccelerationRate of change of velocitym/s²
v=ds/dt
a=dv/dt=d²s/dt²

Types of motion

  • Translational: every point has the same translational displacement in ideal rigid-body translation.
  • Rotational: a body turns about an axis.
  • Oscillatory: repeated motion about an equilibrium position.
  • General plane motion: combination of translation and rotation.

5. Work

Work is done when a force causes displacement in the direction of its component. For a constant force:

W=Fs cosθ

where F is force, s is displacement and θ is the angle between them.

ConditionWork
Force and displacement same directionPositive maximum work
Force perpendicular to displacementZero work
Force opposite displacementNegative work

SI unit: joule (J), where 1 J=1 N·m.

6. Energy

Energy is the capacity to do work. In mechanical systems, common forms include kinetic energy, potential energy and energy associated with thermal or fluid systems.

Kinetic energy

KE=½mv²

Gravitational potential energy

PE=mgh

For an ideal isolated mechanical system, energy is conserved even though it may change from one form to another.

Example: When a mass falls, gravitational potential energy decreases while kinetic energy increases, neglecting losses. This illustrates energy conversion.

7. Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

P=dW/dt

For constant force and velocity in the same direction, P=Fv. The SI unit is watt (W), with 1 W=1 J/s.

Dimensional homogeneity

An equation is dimensionally homogeneous when every term has the same dimensions. Dimensional analysis is useful for checking equations and converting between unit systems, although it cannot by itself determine dimensionless numerical constants.

9. SI System and Unit Conversion

The SI system provides a consistent international system of units. Engineering calculations should use consistent units before substituting values into formulas.

Conversion workflow: write the given quantity → write the conversion factor → cancel units → verify the final unit.
Example: 1 kW=1000 W. Therefore 2.5 kW = 2500 W.

10. Engineering Ethics and Professionalism

Engineering decisions can directly affect public safety, the environment, property and society. Professional engineering therefore requires technical competence together with ethical responsibility.

Core professional principles

  • Protect public safety and welfare.
  • Be honest and objective in technical communication.
  • Work within one's competence and seek expert support when necessary.
  • Respect confidentiality and intellectual property.
  • Avoid conflicts of interest or disclose them appropriately.
  • Follow applicable standards, regulations and safety requirements.
  • Consider environmental and societal impacts.
  • Maintain professional knowledge through continuous learning.
12-mark writing tip: Connect ethics to a real engineering decision: identify the technical issue → identify who may be affected → explain the safety/professional responsibility → state the responsible action.

11. Engineering Example — Force, Work and Power

A machine applies a constant force of 500 N and moves a load 4 m in the same direction in 8 s.

Work: W=Fs=500×4=2000 J

Average power: P=W/t=2000/8=250 W

This links the three basic ideas: force causes displacement, displacement under force gives work, and the rate of doing work gives power.

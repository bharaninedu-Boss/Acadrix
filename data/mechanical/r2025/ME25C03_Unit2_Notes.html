1. Why Mechanics of Materials Matters

Mechanics of Materials studies how engineering members respond when forces are applied. A designer must ensure that a component is strong enough, sufficiently stiff and safe under the expected loading. The basic ideas in this unit support later study of Strength of Materials, machine design and structural design.

2. Stress

2.1 Meaning

Stress is the internal resisting force developed per unit area when an external load acts on a body.

σ=P/A

Here P is the axial load and A is the original cross-sectional area. Tensile loading produces tensile stress; compressive loading produces compressive stress.

2.2 Shear Stress

When the force acts tangentially to an area, the resulting average stress is shear stress:

τ=V/A

where V is the shear force acting parallel to the resisting area.

3. Strain

Strain measures deformation relative to the original dimension. It is dimensionless.

ε=ΔL/L

For shear deformation, shear strain is represented by γ and is related to angular distortion for small deformation.

4. Elasticity and Plasticity

4.1 Elasticity

Elasticity is the ability of a material to return approximately to its original shape after the load is removed, provided the material remains within its elastic range.

4.2 Plasticity

Plasticity is the ability to undergo permanent deformation after the elastic limit is exceeded. The component does not completely return to its original shape after unloading.

Easy memory: Elastic deformation is recoverable; plastic deformation is permanent.

5. Stress-Strain Behaviour

A tensile test provides a useful way to understand material behaviour. A typical engineering stress-strain curve can be discussed in terms of the initial elastic region, yielding, plastic deformation, ultimate tensile strength and fracture. The exact shape differs between materials.

  • Elastic region: deformation is substantially recoverable.
  • Yielding: significant plastic deformation begins.
  • Ultimate tensile strength: maximum engineering stress reached in the tensile test.
  • Fracture: final failure of the specimen.

7. Axial Loading

An axial load acts along the longitudinal axis of a member. For a uniform member under a concentric axial force, average normal stress is σ=P/A.

Numerical workflow: identify P → determine the resisting area → convert units consistently → calculate σ → state whether the stress is tensile or compressive.
Example: A bar of area 200 mm² carries a tensile load of 20 kN. σ=20,000/200=100 N/mm²=100 MPa.

8. Bending

Bending occurs when loads produce a bending moment that tends to curve a member. Beams are common examples.

In elementary beam theory, bending produces tensile stress on one side and compressive stress on the other, with a neutral axis between them. The magnitude varies with distance from the neutral axis.

σ=My/I

where M is bending moment, y is distance from the neutral axis and I is the second moment of area about the relevant neutral axis.

Key points

  • Stress is zero at the neutral axis in the idealised pure-bending model.
  • Stress magnitude increases with distance from the neutral axis.
  • Cross-sectional geometry strongly affects bending resistance through I.

9. Torsion

Torsion is twisting produced by an applied torque. Circular shafts are common torsional members.

τ=Tρ/J

Here T is torque, ρ is radial distance from the shaft axis and J is the polar second moment of area. The maximum shear stress occurs at the outer surface of a circular shaft in the standard elastic torsion model.

10. Shear Force and Bending Moment — Basic Idea

For a beam carrying transverse loads, the internal shear force and bending moment describe how the beam resists the applied loads.

  • Shear force: internal force associated with transverse loading.
  • Bending moment: internal moment that causes bending.
  • Free-body diagrams help identify reactions and internal actions.
  • Sign conventions must be kept consistent throughout a solution.

11. Basic Structural Analysis and Design

Structural analysis determines the internal forces, reactions and deformations produced by loads. Structural design then uses these results to select dimensions and materials that satisfy strength, stiffness, stability, safety and practical requirements.

Simple design sequence

1. Identify loads and supports.
2. Draw the free-body diagram.
3. Determine support reactions.
4. Determine relevant internal forces/moments.
5. Evaluate stresses and deformation as required.
6. Compare with suitable allowable/design criteria.
7. Select or revise the section/material and check again.

12. Common Engineering Applications

  • Design of shafts, beams and structural members.
  • Selection of materials for machine components.
  • Checking whether a component can safely carry an applied load.
  • Choosing cross-sections to control bending stress and deflection.
  • Designing members for torsional loads.
  • Understanding failure and deformation during manufacturing and service.

17. Formula & Quick Revision

ε=ΔL/L — normal strain
τ=V/A — average shear stress
σ=My/I — elementary bending stress relation
τ=Tρ/J — elementary circular-shaft torsion relation
  • Tension tends to elongate; compression tends to shorten.
  • Elastic deformation is recoverable within the elastic range.
  • Plastic deformation is permanent.
  • Toughness concerns energy absorption before fracture.
  • Hardness concerns resistance to indentation/scratching/wear.
  • Always draw a free-body diagram before analysing a loaded structure.

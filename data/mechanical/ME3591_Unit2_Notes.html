1. Unit II syllabus map

Unit II covers shafts and axles; solid and hollow shafts based on strength, rigidity and critical speed; keys and splines; rigid couplings; and flexible couplings.

2. Shafts and axles

A shaft is a rotating or stationary circular member that supports elements such as gears, pulleys, flywheels and sprockets. A transmission shaft primarily transmits power and normally experiences bending and torsion. An axle is generally stationary and is mainly subjected to bending.

For shaft design, the three main checks are:

  1. Strength — stresses must remain within the design stress.
  2. Torsional rigidity — angle of twist must be acceptable.
  3. Lateral rigidity — transverse deflection must be acceptable.

Exam idea: Identify all forces and torques → find the critical section → calculate bending moment and torque → design diameter from strength → check rigidity → select a standard size.

3. Torque from transmitted power

When power P is given in kW and speed N is in rpm:

T = 9.55 × 10³ P / N
where T is in N·m.

For N·mm:

T = 9.55 × 10⁶ P / N
where T is in N·mm.

Keep power, speed and torque units consistent.

4. Shaft stresses

4.1 Bending stress

σb = 32M / (πd³)

4.2 Torsional shear stress

τ = 16T / (πd³)

When bending and torsion act together, both stresses must be considered through an appropriate failure theory.

5. Combined bending and torsion

Bhandari uses equivalent moments according to the selected failure theory.

Equivalent twisting moment by maximum-shear-stress theory:
Te = √(M² + T²)
Equivalent bending moment by maximum-principal-stress theory:
Me = 1/2 [ M + √(M² + T²) ]
Remember: Do not add M and T directly. They are different loading modes.

6. Design stress and ASME-style shaft design

Bhandari gives material-based shaft design stresses. A commonly used design shear stress is selected as the lower of the two material limits:

Ssd = min(0.18 Sut, 0.30 Syt)

For combined loading, use the correction/service factors stated in the problem. Do not invent a factor when the question provides one.

7. Shaft rigidity

7.1 Torsional rigidity

θ = TL / (GJ)

For a solid circular shaft:

J = πd⁴ / 32

The diameter has a fourth-power effect on torsional rigidity.

Bhandari notes typical limits such as 1° twist over a length of 20d for a line shaft; specific applications may specify other limits.

7.2 Lateral rigidity

For lateral rigidity, treat the shaft as a beam supported by bearings and loaded by the actual gear, pulley and other forces. Find the maximum deflection and compare it with the permitted value.

8. Critical speed

Critical speed, or whirling speed, is the speed at which the rotating shaft approaches a resonance condition because of lateral vibration. It becomes important when rotating masses such as gears and pulleys are mounted on the shaft.

Exam rule: Keep operating speed safely away from the critical speed. For a numerical critical-speed question, use the method and constants specified in the prescribed text/data available for the examination.

For a hollow shaft, K = Di/Do.

J = (π/32) Do⁴(1 − K⁴)

Because material is placed farther from the axis, a hollow shaft can provide better torsional performance for the same weight and length when properly proportioned.

10. Forces acting on shafts

Belt drive

The shaft receives the tight-side and slack-side belt pulls. Their difference produces torque and their combined pull produces a bending load.

T = (T1 − T2)D/2

The radial pull on the shaft is based on the actual belt arrangement and should be resolved into the appropriate planes before drawing bending-moment diagrams.

Gear drive

For a spur gear, the tangential force transmits torque and the radial force adds bending.

FR = FT tan ψ
F = √(FT² + FR²) = FT sec ψ

11. Keys

A key connects a hub to a shaft and transmits torque. Bhandari groups keys into sunk keys and saddle keys. Sunk keys include ordinary, feather, Woodruff and related forms.

Two main failure checks are required:

  1. Shear failure
  2. Crushing failure
Torque by key shear: T = τallow b l d / 2
Torque by key crushing: T = σc,allow h l d / 4

Design sequence: calculate torque → select standard b and h → calculate l from shear → calculate l from crushing → take the larger length → choose a practical standard length.

12. Splines

Splines are multiple integral keys on a shaft that mate with corresponding grooves in a hub. Their larger working surface gives higher torque capacity and more uniform pressure distribution than a single key. They also help centre and guide the hub.

Bhandari discusses parallel-sided splines and involute splines. Parallel splines are specified using the number of splines and minor/major diameters. Involute splines have gear-like tooth profiles and offer useful strength and manufacturing advantages.

13. Couplings

A coupling joins two rotating shafts. Its purposes include torque transmission, convenient disconnection for maintenance, accommodating permitted misalignment, reducing shock transmission and providing overload protection.

13.1 Rigid couplings

  • Sleeve or muff coupling
  • Clamp or split-muff coupling
  • Protected flange coupling
  • Claw/star coupling

Rigid couplings are intended for properly aligned shafts.

13.2 Flexible couplings

  • Bushed-pin coupling
  • Universal coupling
  • Oldham coupling
  • Spring coupling
  • Tyre coupling

Flexible couplings permit specified lateral/angular misalignment and help absorb shock.

14. Muff coupling — proportions and checks

Bhandari gives usual proportions for a sleeve/muff coupling:

Outside diameter: d₁ ≈ 1.7d + 20 mm to 2d + 15 mm
Length: L ≈ 3.5d to 4d

The sleeve is checked as a hollow shaft. The key is checked for shear and crushing.

For standard dimensions, use the prescribed PSG Design Data Book in the examination rather than inventing a key size.

15. Flange and flexible couplings

For a flange coupling, check the shaft, key, bolts, hub and flange. Typical failure checks include shaft/key shear and crushing, bolt shear/crushing, flange shear and hub shear.

For a bushed-pin flexible coupling, additionally check rubber-bush bearing pressure and pin bending/shear.

16. Unit II exam checklist

  • Identify shaft/axle and all applied loads.
  • Resolve loads into vertical and horizontal planes when necessary.
  • Find bearing reactions before bending moments.
  • Find torque from power and speed with consistent units.
  • Use the stated service/shock factors and failure theory.
  • Design strength first, then check rigidity and critical speed.
  • For hollow shafts, clearly distinguish Do and Di.
  • For keys, check both shear and crushing.
  • For splines, know types, terminology and basic design checks.
  • For couplings, identify rigid/flexible type before using proportions.
  • Round calculated dimensions to practical standard sizes and re-check.
